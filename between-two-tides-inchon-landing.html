Between Two Tides: How the Inchon Landing Worked

In September 1950, an amphibious landing behind North Korean lines offered a way to reverse the opening campaign of the Korean War. To seize Inchon, Marine and Navy planners had to fit their assault into two brief periods of high water.

Introduction

The Korean War began on 25 June 1950, when North Korean forces crossed the 38th parallel and invaded South Korea. The peninsula had been divided into Soviet and American occupation zones after Japan's defeat in 1945, and by 1948 separate governments had emerged on either side of the dividing line. The invasion brought this unresolved division into open war. With United Nations backing, the United States and other countries came to South Korea's defense, but the first American reinforcements could not halt the North Korean advance. By August, South Korean troops and the U.S. Eighth Army were defending the Pusan Perimeter, a shrinking foothold around the southeastern port now known as Busan. Holding that port kept reinforcements and supplies flowing into a country whose capital, Seoul, was already in enemy hands.1

General Douglas MacArthur, commander of United Nations forces, sought to relieve that pressure by bringing troops ashore behind the North Korean lines. His chosen objective was Inchon, now Incheon, on Korea's western coast beside the Yellow Sea. From there, his troops could advance on nearby Seoul and sever the roads and railways supplying the North Korean army in the south. Operation Chromite, as the landing was named, would combine this attack with an Eighth Army offensive from Pusan. Its success depended on getting a substantial force through a harbor whose narrow channels, broad mud flats, and steep sea walls made it a particularly difficult place to land.2

Those difficulties became immediate concerns before dawn on 15 September, as eighteen ships entered Flying Fish Channel in single file on their approach to Inchon. A damaged vessel could obstruct the narrow passage, while guns on Wolmi-do, the island overlooking the inner harbor, threatened the arriving craft. A lighthouse restored by a reconnaissance party helped guide the column toward its first objective: Marines would seize Wolmi-do on the morning tide, then hold it while the main force waited offshore for the evening tide to reach the city. Carrying out that sequence required the experience of Navy and Marine staffs, the work of Korean reconnaissance partners, and the judgment of crews and troops facing conditions that no written order could fully anticipate. Their preparations explain how MacArthur's ambitious choice became a successful landing.3

Why Inchon?

MacArthur had begun considering a landing behind the enemy in early July, while American and South Korean forces were still retreating. His first scheme, Operation Bluehearts, had to be abandoned when troops intended for the assault were committed to the fighting on land, but his Far East Command staff continued planning. On 23 July it circulated the Chromite concept, linking a landing near Seoul with an eventual offensive from the south. Later accounts often treated the resulting plans as competing choices among landing sites. Historian Seo Chijong's study instead emphasizes how the plans worked together: the principal Inchon plan, 100-B, was accompanied by provisions for different developments in Eighth Army's advance and for an unsuccessful or abandoned landing.4

From Inchon, MacArthur's forces could advance on Kimpo airfield and then Seoul, about eighteen miles inland, to recover South Korea's capital and threaten the transport network supporting the invasion. MacArthur assigned the advance to X Corps, a headquarters under Major General Edward M. Almond that would control the 1st Marine Division and the Army's 7th Infantry Division. If these forces took Inchon, secured Kimpo, and crossed the Han River into Seoul while Eighth Army attacked from Pusan, North Korean formations could be cut off from their supplies and routes of retreat. Kunsan, another port roughly one hundred air miles south of Inchon, offered easier approaches from the sea but lay farther from the capital.5

Naval officers who favored an easier landing site feared that Inchon's approaches could cost the force ships and troops before it established a foothold. At a late-August briefing, naval officers described restricted approaches, uncertain charts, strong currents, possible mines, and sea walls where an assault force needed beaches. Rear Admiral James H. Doyle, whose Amphibious Group One would carry and land the troops, had investigated Kunsan as an alternative. MacArthur argued that Inchon's difficulties would help make an attack there unexpected, while its proximity to Seoul offered the best prospect of a decisive result before winter. The Joint Chiefs of Staff gave their final approval on 8 September, after seeking reassurance that air and naval support could sustain both the landing and the forces at Pusan.6

With Inchon approved, Doyle and Major General Oliver P. Smith, commanding the 1st Marine Division, had to work out how to get the assault troops ashore and keep them supplied. Vice Admiral Arthur D. Struble, the Seventh Fleet commander, directed the joint task force assembled for the operation. These officers brought extensive experience of World War II amphibious warfare to a plan that required close cooperation among ships, aircraft, and ground forces. MacArthur had selected the objective and accepted the larger operational risk; the commanders responsible for the landing still had to determine what their forces could accomplish within the hours the tides allowed.7

Tides and Landing Sites

At Inchon, the difference between high and low water could exceed thirty feet, exposing broad mud flats around the inner channel as the tide receded. Planners initially calculated that twenty-three feet of tide would let landing craft cross the flats, then raised the requirement to twenty-five feet after studying later photographs. The larger tank landing ships needed about twenty-nine feet of tide, a height reached on only a few days each month. On 15 September, high water was predicted for 6:59 in the morning and 7:19 in the evening. A later opportunity would come on 27 September, but its lower tide would offer less favorable conditions for bringing the force ashore.8

To use both high tides, the planners divided the assault into two stages. A reinforced battalion would land on Wolmi-do at 6:30 in the morning, capture the island's defenses, and hold them through the low-water interval with naval and air support. The main landing would begin at 5:30 in the afternoon, allowing the troops some daylight before the evening high tide. The 5th Marines, a regiment of Smith's division, would land at Red Beach beside the center of Inchon, while the 1st Marines landed at Blue Beach south of the city, nearer the routes to Seoul. Separated by several miles, the two regiments would initially have to secure their own positions before joining inland.9

Even when the water rose, the men approaching Red Beach would face a stone sea wall that stood sixteen feet above the mud at low tide and would still rise above the bows of their boats. To climb it, Marines were issued wooden and aluminum ladders, grappling hooks, lines, and cargo nets. At Blue Beach, the approach crossed almost two miles of flooded mud flats, so tracked amphibious vehicles, capable of carrying troops through water and onto land, would lead the assault. Delays at either beach could leave boats stranded in exposed mud as the tide fell, making the delivery of tanks, artillery, and ammunition almost as urgent as landing the first infantry.10

Preparing the Landing

Smith's staff began solving these problems while much of the division was still scattered across the Pacific. Some officers reached Tokyo in the second half of August; others remained with Marines fighting near Pusan or with units embarking in California. Struble's fleet staff arrived after Doyle's amphibious staff had begun detailed work, and the division's relationship with Almond's newly organized X Corps was not always clear. Working together aboard the command ship Mount McKinley, the Marine and amphibious staffs could draw on familiar procedures and wartime experience while their higher headquarters continued to settle responsibilities.11

Within twelve days, the division staff produced the operations and administrative orders that would guide the landing. A short main order assigned missions, while eighty-four pages of operational annexes supplied details of the forces, intelligence, maps, and landing sequence. Another fifty-two pages of administrative annexes covered such needs as medical care, engineering, supply handling, and port development. Intelligence officers' assessments of the shore helped operations officers determine the equipment and timing required to reach it. Supply officers, meanwhile, decided which units needed to be combat-loaded, with men and equipment arranged aboard ship in the order they would be needed ashore, and which could follow later. These decisions gave subordinate commanders practical instructions they could use despite the brief preparation time.12

To check the surveys and aerial photographs on which much of that planning depended, Navy Lieutenant Eugene F. Clark and a small American and Korean team worked from Yonghung-do, an island near the harbor approaches. With local help, they examined channels, observed defenses, and tested the mud. Clark reported that Japanese tide tables were more accurate than American ones, that the sea walls were higher than expected, and that Wolmi-do was heavily fortified. His party also repaired the Palmi-do lighthouse shortly before the landing, providing a navigational aid for the approaching ships. The islanders who helped the mission faced grave danger: after Clark's party left Yonghung-do on 14 September, North Korean troops overran its local defenders and killed more than fifty men, women, and children.13

While Clark's reports improved knowledge of the objective, officers assembling the force had to contend with storms and equipment shortages. Typhoons disrupted loading at Kobe in Japan and threatened ships at sea, while some landing vehicles had been hurriedly drawn from storage and lacked trained crews or adequate communications. The discovery of mines in Flying Fish Channel added another concern when available minesweepers were still escorting convoys. Mechanics repaired vehicles, supply personnel rearranged cargoes, and commanders adjusted their organizations as the departure date approached. Enough of the force was ready to sail for the scheduled landing, although several of these weaknesses would reappear during the approach to the beaches.14

Bombarding the Harbor Defenses

Before the troop transports could enter the harbor, aircraft and warships attacked the positions from which North Korean defenders might fire into the landing craft. On 10 September, carrier aircraft struck Wolmi-do with napalm, bombs, rockets, and cannon fire. Three days later, destroyers approached close enough to draw fire from the island's batteries and expose their locations. North Korean shells struck several ships and killed an American officer, while cruisers and destroyers answered with sustained gunfire. The ships returned the next day, joining aircraft in further attacks on the harbor defenses.15

These attacks made a landing increasingly difficult to conceal, but Doyle's force needed to silence the guns overlooking the narrow approaches. A battery that survived could hit boats unable to disperse freely or stop Marines below a sea wall. The bombardment therefore reduced the danger to the assault troops while giving the defenders warning of the area under threat. Its effectiveness would be tested first on Wolmi-do, where the morning landing had to secure the harbor entrance before the larger force could approach Inchon.16

The Morning and Evening Landings

At 5:20 on 15 September, Doyle ordered the landing force ashore, beginning the movement toward Wolmi-do under covering fire from ships and aircraft. The first waves arrived at 6:33, and Marines climbed out of their boats without opposition at the waterline before encountering scattered resistance from caves and prepared positions. By 8:00, the battalion had secured the island and its causeway at a cost of seventeen wounded. As the tide receded, the Marines strengthened their positions while the main landing force waited offshore for the water to rise again.17

Throughout the interval, ships and aircraft continued attacking mainland positions, roads, and suspected reinforcements. North Korean commanders began moving troops toward Inchon, but the reinforcements could not arrive in time to confront the evening assault in strength. Aboard the waiting ships, Marines prepared to cross a harbor increasingly obscured by smoke and rain. Twenty minutes before the scheduled landing, the supporting bombardment intensified as the boats made their final approach.18

The first craft reached Red Beach's sea wall at 5:31, where Marines threw grenades over the top, raised ladders, and climbed into fire from surviving positions. Some boats had arrived at the wrong place, and units became intermingled as smoke hid the hills they had been ordered to capture. In the early fighting near Cemetery Hill, eight Marines were killed and twenty-eight wounded. Small groups nevertheless worked around defended positions and took the high ground, giving the following waves room to land and organize.19

South of the city, more than 170 amphibious tractors were approaching Blue Beach with only four guide boats to help them keep their formations on course. Rainsqualls, smoke, and an unexpected crosscurrent scattered later waves, while inexperienced drivers struggled with vehicles that sometimes lacked radios or compasses. Officers and crews redirected them toward usable exits, but most of one battalion landed about two miles to the left of its assigned beach. The regiment still established itself inland and secured the hills covering the southern approach, recovering enough of its organization to continue toward its objectives.20

By 1:30 the following morning, the landing force held its final objectives for the first day. Red Beach had brought troops into Inchon and onto the hills above it, while Blue Beach had opened an approach to the Seoul road from the south. About two thousand North Korean troops had defended the area; for the joint task force, naval historian Curtis Utz records twenty-one killed, one missing, and 174 wounded. Behind the infantry, sailors and shore parties were still unloading the equipment and ammunition needed to hold the beachhead and begin the advance inland.21

Supplying the Beachhead

To sustain the troops through the night, eight tank landing ships had followed the assault waves to Red Beach carrying fuel, food, vehicles, and ammunition. The loads were distributed so that losing one ship would not deprive the force of an entire category of supplies. Their captains deliberately grounded the ships against the shore and accepted that the falling tide would leave them exposed until morning. North Korean fire struck the hulls, and uncontrolled firing from several ships also killed one Marine and wounded twenty-three ashore. Under floodlights, sailors and Marines continued unloading alongside Seabees, the Navy's construction troops.22

By the morning of 16 September, the ships had delivered about four thousand tons of material and could withdraw on the returning tide, making room for a second group. The Seabees' pontoon causeway at Wolmi-do provided another route ashore, while shore parties organized the supplies and Army engineers assumed responsibility for port operations. Until damaged facilities could be restored, the assault beaches and temporary structures had to carry much of the traffic needed for the advance inland. Securing a harbor was only the beginning of the work required to make it a useful supply base.23

As supplies came ashore, Marine units joined east of Inchon on 16 September, defeated armored counterattacks, and advanced toward Kimpo. They secured the airfield on the morning of the 18th, allowing aircraft to begin using it that afternoon; by the following night the 5th Marines held the south bank of the Han in their sector. Army troops arriving through Inchon extended the advance on the southern flank. Meanwhile, Eighth Army's offensive from the Pusan Perimeter kept pressure on North Korean formations in the south, bringing the two parts of MacArthur's plan into action.24

North Korean Warning and Response

The small garrison and slow arrival of reinforcements helped make the landing possible, but they do not mean that North Korean leaders had never considered an attack at Inchon. American accounts that described complete surprise emphasized the defenders' lack of readiness when the Marines arrived. Yet ships and troops had assembled openly in Japan and Korea, journalists had speculated about their destination, and the bombardment had given local commanders an unmistakable warning. The question is how much the North Koreans knew and whether they could act on it in time.25

Historian Kim Kwang-Soo, examining North Korean and former Soviet records, argues that North Korean leaders had strengthened coastal defenses against possible landings and had considered Inchon among the likely objectives. They nevertheless lacked definite information about the landing site and spread their defensive efforts along the coast while concentrating combat power for another offensive at Pusan. Even after the landing began, they responded too slowly to protect their army in the south. Kim's reconstruction helps explain how advance warning could coexist with an inadequate defense: commanders recognized the danger without putting sufficient troops at the threatened point.26

Deception near Kunsan and the difficulty of Inchon's approaches added to that uncertainty, but the outcome also depended on North Korean deployments and decisions. The defenders left Flying Fish Channel incompletely mined, held the port with a limited force, and failed to reinforce it effectively once the attack developed. United Nations control of the sea and air gave the assault force freedom to exploit these weaknesses. The landing thus benefited from surprise in its practical effects, even though the possibility of an attack had not escaped the enemy's attention.27

Victory and Its Shadow

In its October report, the United Nations Command described the Wolmi-do assault as “perfectly co-ordinated” and presented the recapture of Seoul's communications as confirmation of the campaign plan. By then, United Nations forces held the port and airfield, Eighth Army had broken out of the Pusan Perimeter, and North Korean formations were retreating. For troops who had spent the summer giving ground, these were substantial gains. The report's confident account nevertheless gave little sense of the improvisation at the beaches or the uncertainties that commanders had faced along the way.28

The advance from Inchon to Seoul required further hard fighting through prepared positions, across the Han, and into the capital, where combat continued through 28 September. Historians disagree about whether Smith's methodical advance gave North Korean forces too much time to recover. In his survey of the scholarship, historian Zachary Matusheski describes retired Marine colonel Russel Stolfi's argument that a faster advance could have produced a more complete victory, while questioning Stolfi's comparison with German armored operations in 1941. The campaign required commanders to judge how quickly they could advance while securing the beachhead, bringing up supplies, and overcoming resistance that strengthened nearer Seoul. Success at the landing sites did not remove those demands.29

For civilians on Wolmi-do, the fighting had brought losses that became the subject of a later South Korean investigation. In 2008, the Republic of Korea's Truth and Reconciliation Commission reported that the air attack of 10 September had destroyed a civilian settlement on the island's eastern side. It identified ten civilians who had been killed and estimated that the total, including people whose identities could no longer be established, might have reached about one hundred. The commission also reported finding no evidence of measures to reduce civilian casualties. The estimate reflects the difficulty of reconstructing the losses more than half a century after the attack.30

The absence of documented precautions does not, by itself, establish everything individual planners considered or demonstrate an intention to kill civilians. The attacks were intended to neutralize the defenses commanding the harbor and protect the approaching landing force; the commission's findings record the harm suffered by people living in the area under attack. Acknowledging those losses allows the landing's military achievement to be understood alongside the experience of the civilians caught in the battle.31

As the war continued, Inchon's success also strengthened MacArthur's authority at a time when the coalition faced decisions beyond the original recovery of South Korea. United Nations forces crossed the 38th parallel and advanced toward the Chinese border before Chinese intervention forced them into retreat. Matusheski identifies the confidence that followed Inchon as part of the setting in which MacArthur became harder to question. That later reversal limits what the September victory can explain about the war as a whole, while leaving its immediate achievement intact: an assault through a difficult harbor had established a new front deep behind the North Korean army.32

Conclusion

When the landing ships withdrew on the morning tide of 16 September, they left the Marines holding a port through which reinforcements could enter the campaign near Seoul. Commanders and staffs had drawn on their experience in the Pacific war to prepare an assault under severe constraints, then relied on crews and troops to adapt when boats strayed or units became scattered. The value of that experience was visible in the ground secured, the supplies unloaded, and the force ready to advance inland. Inchon's lasting place in amphibious history rests on this collective accomplishment: thousands of servicemen turned a difficult landing into an opportunity to recover South Korea.
