Operation Eagle Claw: Why the Rescue Stopped at Desert One

To reach the Americans held hostage in Tehran, Operation Eagle Claw required aircraft and assault troops to meet at a remote strip inside Iran. Five months of preparation left crucial parts of that rendezvous untested.

Introduction

During the night of 24–25 April 1980, American helicopters landed beside Air Force transports on a desert strip southeast of Tehran. The helicopters had crossed Iran from the carrier Nimitz; the transports had brought fuel and the assault force assigned to rescue fifty-three American hostages. At this first rendezvous, code-named Desert One, the rescuers were to transfer to helicopters for the next stage of their journey toward the capital.

Only six of the eight helicopters launched from Nimitz reached the strip, and one of those had a hydraulic fault that could not be repaired there. The remaining five could not carry the force and its equipment as planned. President Jimmy Carter approved the commanders' request to withdraw before a helicopter, repositioning for departure, struck a transport. Five Air Force crewmen and three Marines died in the ensuing fire.1

During the five months before the flight, commanders had built the rescue force from units with little experience of working together. They trained its separate elements intensively, but restricted the exchange of information and rehearsed only portions of the final operation. These decisions preserved secrecy while leaving weather hazards poorly understood and arrangements for the rendezvous insufficiently tested. With just two helicopters beyond the six required for the onward flight, the force had little room to absorb the failures that followed.2

Assembling the Rescue Force

Planning had begun soon after Iranian militants seized the United States embassy in Tehran on 4 November 1979. Two days later, Carter authorized study of military options, although the Joint Chiefs of Staff initially judged a helicopter rescue too uncertain to be viable. By 12 November, they had established a small joint task force under Major General James Vaught, an Army officer. Vaught had to select forces and develop a plan while the administration pursued negotiations for the hostages' release.3

For the embassy assault, Vaught's task force relied on Colonel Charles Beckwith's Delta Force, the Army's new counterterrorist unit. Getting Beckwith's men into Tehran required aircraft with very different capabilities. Air Force MC-130 Combat Talon transports could navigate long routes at night and land on austere strips; Navy RH-53D Sea Stallion helicopters, normally used for minesweeping and flown mostly by Marines, could carry the rescuers and hostages between sites without runways. Air Force Colonel James Kyle coordinated the air mission, while Major John Carney's combat controllers would guide aircraft into the desert strip and direct their movements on the ground.

These officers and their units prepared under restrictions intended to prevent disclosure of the rescue plan. Vaught's staff reported through the chairman of the Joint Chiefs, and normal staff channels were largely excluded. Within the force, participants learned only what planners considered necessary for their own tasks. That limited common briefings and training as well as outside scrutiny. The helicopter contingent, in particular, combined aircraft, crews, and maintenance support without the full command and staff structure of an established operational squadron.4

As preparations continued into April, Carter concluded that negotiations offered little prospect of an early release. At a National Security Council meeting on 11 April, he chose the rescue operation; five days later, he met its senior commanders to discuss the details. He promised them freedom to act within the approved plan and stressed the need to avoid unnecessary Iranian deaths. At that meeting, Carter and national security adviser Zbigniew Brzezinski also agreed to raise the helicopter force from seven to eight. They stopped there because a larger formation might compromise surprise.5

Two Nights Inside Iran

To preserve that surprise, the rescuers would spend a day concealed inside Iran between their approach and the assault. On the first evening, three MC-130s were to fly from Masirah Island, off Oman, to Desert One, more than two hundred miles southeast of Tehran. They would carry Beckwith's force, support personnel, and a road-security team. Three EC-130 transports fitted with large internal fuel bladders would follow to refuel the eight helicopters arriving from Nimitz.

Once refueled, the helicopters would take the assault force to a hiding place outside Tehran, where American agents would meet the troops. The crews would conceal their aircraft at a separate site through the following day. After nightfall, agents would drive Beckwith's men into the capital for the embassy assault, while a smaller Army Special Forces team recovered three diplomats held at the Foreign Ministry. AC-130 gunships would provide support if needed, and the helicopters would return to collect the hostages and rescuers.

Their flight out of Tehran depended on another force reaching Manzariyeh, an airfield south of the city. Army Rangers were to seize it so that two C-141 transports could land and evacuate the hostages, rescuers, and helicopter crews. The helicopters would be destroyed after delivering their passengers. The plan therefore required coordinated movements from the carrier and several air bases, a concealed stay near Tehran, two assaults in the capital, and a separate airfield seizure, all within roughly forty hours.6

As the force assigned to these tasks grew, so did its demand for helicopter lift. More men and equipment had to be carried, while rising seasonal temperatures reduced aircraft performance. By April, six helicopters had to leave Desert One for the operation to proceed as approved. Launching eight allowed for two losses before that point. Any further attrition during the rescue could require shuttle flights out of Tehran; rescuers unable to leave by air might have to escape overland.7

Rehearsal and Weather Preparation

While planners adjusted the force and its loads, crews and troops practiced at widely separated sites. C-130 crews rehearsed night approaches and desert landings, helicopter crews learned the long low-level route and ground-refueling procedure, and Beckwith's men practiced their assault. They also faced changing expectations about when they might be ordered to act. Threats to the hostages could require an early attempt, whereas promising diplomatic contacts repeatedly raised hopes that no rescue would be necessary.

The desert refueling procedure received a limited rehearsal on 13–14 April, when two C-130s and four helicopters met at a California dry lake bed. Crews successfully transferred fuel and identified problems with aircraft spacing and the position of a tanker on sloping ground. They did not assemble the full force or reproduce all the movements required at Desert One. No complete rehearsal brought together the transports, helicopters, assault troops, road-security team, and commanders under comparable conditions. The planners accepted the partial exercise as sufficient to confirm the refueling method.8

As the aircrews rehearsed, the task force's weather officer, Captain Donald Buchanan, sought to brief them on the seasonal hazards along their routes into Iran. He and his Air Weather Service colleagues had identified suspended dust as a danger, and information about its occurrence entered a weather annex to the operations plan. Task-force restrictions prevented the direct briefings, however, leaving weather information to pass through intermediaries before reaching the pilots.

Buchanan's forecasters also had few observations from Iran on which to base their predictions. Satellite imagery could not reliably show local dust at night, so a favorable forecast still left considerable uncertainty along the route. The task force had not arranged advance weather reconnaissance to reduce that uncertainty, and the crews remained unprepared for a hazard the forecasters already knew was possible.9

The Flight to Desert One

On the evening of 24 April, the first MC-130 left Masirah with Kyle and Carney aboard. Its crew encountered two broad areas of suspended dust, produced by distant thunderstorms, before reaching clear air near Desert One. The aircraft's navigation equipment helped it continue despite the poor visibility. Kyle considered the first dust cloud too minor to justify breaking radio silence; when the second proved more serious, an attempt to send an encrypted warning failed. The helicopters following a different route received no useful warning of what lay ahead.10

At Desert One, Carney's controllers marked landing areas and began directing the arriving transports. On the road through the site, the security team stopped a bus and detained its passengers, then fired an antitank weapon at a fuel truck whose driver ignored orders to halt. The truck burned beside the site while its driver escaped in a following pickup. Its glare interfered with night-vision equipment as additional C-130s approached to land.11

On the helicopter route, one crew had landed to investigate a warning of possible rotor-blade damage. They abandoned their aircraft and boarded another helicopter. The remaining seven encountered dust that obscured both the ground and neighboring aircraft, broke their formation, and forced some crews to stop and regain their bearings. A helicopter already suffering instrument problems turned back toward Nimitz. Six eventually reached Desert One, separated from one another and substantially behind schedule.12

The crew of one helicopter had pressed on to Desert One with a failed hydraulic system, hoping to have it repaired at the rendezvous. The fault could not be corrected there, and the aircraft was ruled unfit to continue. Kyle asked whether Beckwith could reduce his assault force enough to use the five serviceable helicopters. Beckwith considered the full force necessary, leaving the operation below its approved minimum lift. Kyle recommended cancellation through Vaught's headquarters in Egypt, and Carter accepted the field commanders' decision.13

The Collision and Withdrawal

By the time the abort was approved, the C-130s had spent much longer on the ground than planned and were using fuel needed for their return flight. One tanker, flown by Hal Lewis, needed to leave promptly. Two helicopters stood behind it and had to move before it could depart. Controllers had parked the helicopters close to the transports because the refueling hoses limited the distance between them.

Moving aircraft through those confined spaces required orders to pass among men who could barely hear or recognize one another. Kyle and Beckwith often had to confer face to face, as did the leaders of the helicopter and transport elements. Carney's controllers could contact aircraft by radio, but no common net connected all the air and ground elements, and the on-scene commander had no clearly marked command post. Some helicopter pilots later told investigators that they had not recognized the authority of officers issuing orders at the site.14

To clear Lewis's tanker, one helicopter had to lift off rather than taxi over the soft ground. Its downwash raised a dust cloud that hid the pilot's outside references, and the aircraft drifted into the EC-130. Fire engulfed both aircraft, killing five men in the transport and three in the helicopter. Other men escaped from the transport through a rear side door, and two soldiers went back into the burning aircraft to pull its radio operator to safety.15

Kyle ordered the helicopters abandoned while Carney's seven controllers and the C-130 loadmasters moved survivors aboard the three transports still at the site and helped account for the force. At Kyle's direction, the controllers also collected the landing lights. With only dimmer markers remaining, two departing C-130s struck a sand berm across their takeoff path, although both became airborne. Kyle and Carney left in the last transport; five helicopters and the bodies of the dead remained behind.16

The Holloway Investigation

Carter publicly accepted responsibility on 25 April, and the Joint Chiefs commissioned an operational review under retired Admiral James L. Holloway III. Its August report examined twenty-three issues, identifying eleven as major. The reviewers traced the abort directly to the combination of helicopter failures and low visibility during the approach. They also examined why the task force had been so vulnerable to those events, including its aircraft reserve, weather arrangements, and incomplete rehearsal.17

Holloway's reviewers found that additional helicopters and crews had been available, and that Nimitz could have carried them without sacrificing other missions. Desert One's fuel supply could also have supported a larger force, though with less contingency fuel remaining. The principal objection had been the threat that additional aircraft posed to secrecy throughout preparation and execution. Planners had accepted a smaller allowance for breakdowns to limit that exposure. Holloway's group judged the tradeoff too restrictive, while acknowledging that extra helicopters could not have guaranteed success.18

The reviewers also called for rehearsing the crowded refueling operation with the full force, so its commanders could test their communications and control arrangements under the noise and dust of execution. Retaining an established helicopter unit's staff could have improved the exchange of maintenance and operational information. Later, Army officer William Flynt placed particular weight on divided tactical command in his study of the mission. Holloway's broader review found recurring problems in both the temporary organization and the restrictions imposed for security, while still judging the rescue concept feasible.

Although the reviewers considered the rescue feasible, they could not precisely estimate its chances once fighting began in Tehran. The response of guards and military units would have complicated the search for hostages and their removal from the city. The report explicitly treated estimates of success in that unexecuted phase as conjectural. More aircraft and better preparation could have reduced the risk of an early abort; they could not settle what would have happened at the embassy.19

Conclusion

After Desert One, Holloway recommended giving a permanent joint headquarters responsibility for planning and training a counterterrorist force. With an assigned staff and forces, the headquarters could develop working relationships and rehearse joint operations before a crisis imposed its own deadline. An independent advisory panel would scrutinize its plans. The Defense Department adopted both recommendations; Joint Special Operations Command became operational in December 1980.20
