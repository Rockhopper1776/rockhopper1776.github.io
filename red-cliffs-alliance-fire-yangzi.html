Red Cliffs, AD 208: Alliance and Fire on the Yangzi

The battle of Red Cliffs took place on the middle Yangzi (Yangtze) River in what is now Hubei Province in central China. When the Han chancellor Cao Cao brought a captured fleet down the river, Sun Quan, who ruled farther east, had to decide whether to submit or send his own ships upstream.

Introduction

By AD 208, civil war had left much of China's Han empire in the hands of regional commanders. Cao Cao, a Han officer who raised troops of his own during the war, brought Emperor Xian and the imperial court to his headquarters in AD 196. He went on to defeat his chief rivals and take control of most of northern China. When Cao Cao turned south as the emperor's chief minister, he could demand submission in the name of the Han.1

Sun Quan was still in his teens when his older brother Sun Ce was killed in AD 200, leaving him an army and territory along the lower Yangzi. After securing those holdings, Sun Quan sent his forces upriver against Liu Biao, the governor of Jing, which straddled the middle Yangzi. Liu Biao had given refuge to Liu Bei, a general who had once fought alongside Cao Cao before the two became enemies.1

Cao Cao's advance into Jing drove Liu Bei to seek Sun Quan's help. When their combined forces met Cao Cao at Red Cliffs late in AD 208, disease and fatigue had already weakened his army, and an attack with burning ships helped force it to retreat. Later retellings, including the Chinese novel Romance of the Three Kingdoms, made much of Cao Cao's losses. The early accounts leave the scale of those losses uncertain, so the following year's campaign offers a firmer measure of what Sun Quan and Liu Bei gained.2

Cao Cao Reaches the Yangzi

When Liu Biao died as the northern army approached Jing, his younger son and successor, Liu Cong, surrendered the province. Liu Bei fled south with troops and civilians. Cao Cao's cavalry overtook the column near Changban and defeated Liu Bei's troops, though Liu Bei himself escaped and rejoined a fleet commanded by his general Guan Yu.

The same rapid movement gave Cao Cao Jiangling, an important base with an existing river force. It also carried his army far from northern China after a long season of campaigning. Men accustomed to land operations now depended on waterways; many recently surrendered soldiers and sailors had served Liu Biao only weeks before, and disease spread through the force. These burdens reduced the advantage suggested by Cao Cao's larger resources without making defeat inevitable.3

From Jiangling, Cao Cao sent Sun Quan a letter claiming a force of eight hundred thousand men, a boast preserved in the chronicle of the eleventh-century historian Sima Guang. Whatever the true number, Cao Cao now held a major river base and many of Jing's ships. Some of Sun Quan's advisers urged submission.3

Sun Quan Chooses Resistance

Before that debate was settled, Sun Quan's adviser Lu Su had gone west to learn what had happened in Jing. He met Liu Bei after the defeat at Changban and urged cooperation. Liu Bei sent his own adviser, Zhuge Liang, to seek an alliance with Sun Quan, whose territory lay in the path of Cao Cao's advance.4

Later histories differ on who persuaded Sun Quan to fight, giving different weight to the advice of Zhuge Liang, Lu Su, and Zhou Yu, a general who had served both Sun brothers. Sun Quan put Zhou Yu and another veteran commander, Cheng Pu, in charge of the expedition. Liu Bei and Liu Cong's elder brother, Liu Qi, brought troops of their own, though their numbers are uncertain.4

In the accounts of the debate, Zhou Yu conceded that Cao Cao had the larger army and pointed to the conditions in which it had to fight. Winter limited grazing for the army's horses, disease had already appeared, and many northern troops had little experience on the waterways. Zhou Yu also doubted the loyalty of men who had lately served Liu Biao. Sun Quan sent his fleet upstream and kept a reserve at home.4

The Fleets Meet at Red Cliffs

Zhou Yu's force joined Liu Bei and moved upriver past the point where the Han River flows into the Yangzi at present-day Wuhan as Cao Cao came downriver from Jiangling. The early histories place the first encounter at Red Cliffs and Cao Cao's camp near Wulin. The exact location of the fighting remains disputed.5

In the first clash, Cao Cao's men failed to break the allies and withdrew to the north bank. The sources mention fatigue and disease without saying how long the pause lasted. They describe Cao Cao's ships held close together, though the way they were secured is uncertain. Huang Gai, one of Zhou Yu's officers, judged that fire could spread from one ship to the next.5

Fire was familiar on a wooden fleet, so Huang Gai's plan depended on surprise rather than novelty. He proposed approaching under a message of surrender, which would allow prepared vessels to close before Cao Cao's men recognized the attack. The plan joined a familiar method to a particular opportunity: crowded ships, a favorable wind, and an enemy willing to receive a defector.6

Huang Gai's Fire Attack

The chronicle describes ten ships loaded with fuel soaked in oil and covered over; other traditions say only that several tens of vessels were prepared, with small boats fastened behind for the crews' escape. As Huang Gai's group approached, the northern force watched what appeared to be the promised surrender. The attackers then set the ships alight and withdrew into the boats.6

Driven by a southeast wind, the burning vessels struck Cao Cao's concentration and carried fire among ships and camps along the bank. Zhou Yu's troops attacked behind the fireships. With the fleet and shore position disordered, Cao Cao abandoned the confrontation and took the inland route toward Jiangling.6

The victors' accounts describe heavy destruction and pursuit, while the tradition attached to Cao Cao says that epidemic disease had already made retreat necessary and that he burned his own remaining ships. Those accounts may describe different stages of the same retreat. Sickness weakened the army; Huang Gai's attack struck it during that weakness; and Cao Cao may have destroyed vessels he could not safely remove. No surviving evidence permits a dependable casualty total.7

Victory and Its Limits

As Cao Cao turned back north, he left his generals Cao Ren and Xu Huang at Jiangling and Yue Jin farther north at Xiangyang, on the Han River. Zhou Yu and Liu Bei pursued the retreating army, and Zhou Yu took Jiangling in AD 209 after a prolonged campaign.8

From Xiangyang, Cao Cao still held the route into northern China up the Han River. Sun Quan kept his lands on the lower Yangzi, and Liu Bei established himself in southern Jing before moving west into Yi Province, centered on what is now Sichuan.9

The allies soon quarreled over Jing, and their armies eventually fought each other. Sun Quan went on to rule Wu along the Yangzi, and Liu Bei founded Shu-Han in the southwest. To the north, Cao Cao's family ruled Wei. These states are known as the Three Kingdoms.9

Conclusion

The early accounts are strongest on sequence: Sun Quan sent a force upriver, Huang Gai's approach burned Cao Cao's ships and camps, and Cao Cao retreated toward Jiangling. They are weaker on the speeches that assign credit, the size of either army, and the total destruction. Keeping those levels of confidence separate makes the following year, rather than a spectacular casualty figure, the best measure of the victory.10

In AD 220, Cao Cao's son Cao Pi ended the Han dynasty by taking the throne from Emperor Xian. He inherited the northern territory his father had secured, and in AD 222 his armies returned to the Yangzi. Sun Quan's forces still held the river.10
