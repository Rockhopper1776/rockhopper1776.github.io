When the Front Moved: Surprise and Combined Arms at Amiens

On 8 August 1918, Allied troops advanced farther in hours than British armies had often moved in months. The conditions that opened the German line also explain why the advance slowed within days.

Introduction

At 4:20 on the morning of 8 August 1918, more than two thousand British guns opened east of Amiens. There had been no preliminary bombardment to advertise the assault. Infantry moved behind the barrage while tanks crawled out of a ground mist that thickened in the Luce and Somme valleys. By mid-afternoon the Canadian and Australian corps in the center had advanced almost eight miles. German headquarters, batteries, and supply columns that had begun the day behind a settled front found Allied troops among them.1

The movement looked like a technological revelation, and the mass of tanks made that reading tempting. Yet the machines worked within an arrangement that included secrecy, surveyed and predicted artillery fire, counter-battery plans, infantry trained to bypass resistance, aircraft, cavalry, motorized detachments, and staffs able to move fresh divisions through successive objectives. That arrangement did not work equally across the battlefield, nor did it survive unchanged. German reserves arrived, guns found the tanks, roads filled, artillery support lagged, and the advance entered the old Somme battlefield. By 11 August only a fraction of the tanks committed on the first morning remained in action.

Amiens therefore presents two connected questions: why did the front move so quickly on 8 August, and why did it move so much less over the following days? The answer lies neither in tanks alone nor in a sudden collapse of the entire German army. Allied commanders assembled a temporary superiority in information, fire, protection, and practiced formations; when those advantages began to decay, they preserved the initiative by preparing to strike somewhere else.

A Salient and a Sequence

The battlefield had been shaped by Germany's spring offensives. Operation Michael failed to capture Amiens in March and April, leaving General Georg von der Marwitz's Second Army in a broad salient east of the city. The position threatened the Paris–Amiens railway, but it also stretched German forces across ground won at heavy cost. By July the last German offensives had failed, the Allies had stopped the attack on the Marne, and American troops were arriving in growing numbers. The strategic balance had shifted before the first tank started toward its assembly area.2

Marshal Ferdinand Foch intended to use that change through a sequence rather than a single grand rupture. His July program joined limited offensives to free essential railways and deny German reserves time to recover between blows. Fourth Army under General Sir Henry Rawlinson and French First Army under General Marie-Eugène Debeney would clear the Amiens area first; other armies could then attack on different sectors. Foch, Haig, and their staffs still expected the war to continue into 1919, so Amiens was planned as the opening move in a widening campaign, not as a wager that one thrust would reach Germany.3

That larger purpose gave Rawlinson an immediate operational problem. The German salient could be attacked on favorable rolling ground south of the Somme, but only if Fourth Army concentrated enough force without warning Marwitz. Surprise was not an atmospheric gift waiting in the morning fog. It had to be manufactured through movements, fire plans, and explanations convincing enough to mislead observers on both sides of the line.

Building Surprise

The Canadian Corps was the most conspicuous piece to hide. German intelligence associated it with major attacks, and its transfer from the Arras sector could reveal the intended blow. Canadian wireless units therefore remained or appeared elsewhere, while troops moved south largely by night. An Australian division temporarily extended across the ground the Canadians would occupy, making the change look like a routine shortening of the French front. Some Canadian assault units did not enter the forward line until only hours before zero. Commanders also restricted new work that aerial observers might notice, even when the restrictions complicated ammunition supply and reduced the time junior officers had to study the ground.4

Gunners and tank crews extended the deception to the weapons that would support the assault. Artillery crews used survey, calibration, meteorological data, and registration conducted away from the target area so that the guns could fire accurately without ranging shots. Normal harassing fire continued while a large aircraft flew above the forward trenches to cover the sound of tank engines. The tanks themselves approached in low gear from positions behind the line. These measures did not remove friction: fuel was short, roads were crowded, and some ammunition arrived late. They did keep the concentration from producing the long preliminary bombardment and visible traffic that had announced earlier offensives.

Rawlinson and Debeney also worked with unequal resources within the Allied coalition. Fourth Army concentrated the Australian and Canadian corps, British III Corps, most of the Tank Corps, cavalry, and more than two thousand guns. Debeney's First Army attacked on the right with few tanks because French armor had suffered heavily on the Marne. His infantry therefore waited through a short bombardment and advanced at 5:05, forty-five minutes after the British start. The same battle opened on different tactical schedules, a distinction that would shape both its early success and its later interpretation. 5

The Center Breaks Open

As Canadian and Australian infantry approached their start lines, darkness and mist enlarged the secrecy their staffs had created. Twelve minutes before zero the tanks moved; then the barrage broke across the front and the leading infantry followed. In the thickest fog, Canadian units had difficulty maintaining direction and tanks sometimes lost contact with the men they were meant to support. The same fog also blinded German machine gunners and artillery observers. German counter-fire began quickly, but Allied counter-battery work made much of it scattered and ineffective.6

The assault developed as a succession of tasks rather than one continuous wave. Leading battalions moved behind the barrage, marked or attacked machine-gun positions, and left bypassed posts to later troops. Tanks crushed wire, engaged strongpoints, and reached batteries whose crews had little warning that the infantry front had already passed them. Fresh brigades and divisions then crossed the first objectives, while field batteries displaced forward to extend the reach of supporting fire. The Australian and Canadian corps had practiced parts of this method at Hamel and in earlier operations; at Amiens, Fourth Army applied it across a much wider front and to greater depth.

Once the mist lifted, the results in the center became visible. Roads carried prisoners west while cavalry, armored cars, Whippet tanks, and infantry moved east. Some German units continued to fight from woods, villages, and field batteries, but others found Allied troops behind positions they had expected to defend frontally. By afternoon the Australian and Canadian corps had reached or approached the outer Amiens defenses. Their progress showed what combined arms could accomplish when the supporting systems arrived together, but the edges of the attack showed how quickly the relationship could come apart.7

One Battle, Unequal Results

North of the Somme, British III Corps attacked across more difficult ground with troops already unsettled by a German local attack on 6 August. It failed to clear the commanding Chilpilly Spur on schedule, leaving the Australian left exposed and slowing progress along the river. South of the Canadians, French First Army captured its first objectives but advanced on a timetable shaped by its artillery preparation and relative lack of tanks. Debeney's forces later took Montdidier, yet their first morning did not reproduce the deep penetration made in the center.8

Exploitation remained imperfect even within the successful center because horses could outpace Whippets on open ground until they came under fire; tanks could cross ground that stopped cavalry, but they lacked the speed and communication to solve every resistance point. Men carried forward inside Mark V* tanks became sick or fainted from heat, fumes, and violent movement. Near Beaucourt a concealed German battery knocked out ten troop-carrying tanks, and several survivors withdrew when fire stiffened near Le Quesnel. The plan had linked machines and formations with different speeds, endurance, and vulnerabilities. Once the opening timetable slipped, coordination depended increasingly on local decisions.9

The Royal Air Force supplied another warning against a seamless account. Its squadrons had tasks against aerodromes and rail centers, but it diverted major effort to bridges over the Somme. On 8 August its aircraft flew 205 bombing sorties and lost heavily without destroying the spans. German fighters concentrated over the same targets, while the bridges continued to carry reserves toward the battle. Aircraft helped protect the secret concentration and contested the air, but one of the largest air efforts of the day pursued an objective it could not achieve.10

Shock inside Second Army

For the German divisions between the Somme and the Luce, the disaster was severe despite the uneven Allied results elsewhere. Front battalions, supports, local reserves, and artillery were captured or destroyed in large numbers. Tanks appeared beside command posts and batteries before higher headquarters had assembled a usable picture. Units withdrawing through the fog met reserves still moving west, while severed telephone lines and the speed of the advance made orders obsolete. Ludendorff later called 8 August “the black day of the German Army,” not only because ground had been lost but because groups of soldiers had surrendered with a readiness he had not believed possible.11

Ludendorff's verdict needs to be read as testimony from a shaken commander, not as a clinical measure of an entire army. He blamed indiscipline and influences from the home front, although his own failed offensives had consumed men, reserves, and confidence. Bean, drawing on German unit histories, placed surprise first among the immediate causes, followed by tanks and mist, while also emphasizing the strain already imposed on the defenders. Martin Samuels has argued more sharply that fog left forward troops unable to act effectively against tanks and helped produce local panic, whereas German defense recovered as visibility returned. His full argument requires inspection beyond the accessible abstract, but the distinction fits the battlefield: some formations dissolved on the first morning, while others formed a continuous line within a day.12

Second Army's reaction turned local survival into operational recovery. Reserves moved by rail and lorry from neighboring armies, sometimes entering combat without their organic artillery. By 9 August seven reinforcing divisions had reached or were approaching the front. They could not restore the original positions, but they occupied villages, woods, and old trenches beyond the first day's advance. The Allied system had created shock faster than German headquarters could absorb it; the German army then reduced that shock by feeding new units into terrain where the attackers' original arrangements no longer applied.

Why the Advance Slowed

Tanks provide the clearest measure of that decay. Roger Blaber counts 430 engaged on 8 August and only 38 in action on 11 August. Those figures do not mean that nearly four hundred machines were destroyed. Tanks ran out of fuel, broke tracks, ditched, required maintenance, or waited for salvage; crews also suffered from heat, exhaustion, and fumes. Enemy fire nevertheless became increasingly important once surprise had gone. Nicholson records that 145 tanks were fit on the second day and that direct German hits caused most subsequent losses in the Canadian sector. A weapon that had helped infantry cross the first defenses could not simply drive on as an independent spearhead.13

Gunners, transport units, medical services, and headquarters also had to move. Field guns displaced across damaged roads; heavy guns took longer. Ammunition, fuel, food, medical support, and headquarters followed through a landscape crowded with prisoners, cavalry, lorries, and advancing units. On 9 August, changed orders at Fourth Army forced the tired Canadian 3rd Division back into the line and delayed its attack for five hours, giving German defenders more time to prepare. By 10 August Canadian battalions were again fighting wire, trenches, machine guns, and villages, while Australian progress near Lihons lagged and exposed the Canadian flank.14

As these strains accumulated, the ground grew less favorable, German reserves became denser, the attackers' artillery and supplies stretched, tanks disappeared from the order of battle, and the detailed opening plan gave way to improvised attacks. Monash judged that cheap exploitation had ended once surprise was lost, tank resources had diminished, and heavy artillery had not reached new positions. Currie reached the same practical conclusion when he opposed another major assault against the prepared Roye–Chaulnes defenses. Their caution did not reject offensive action; it asked where the Allies could recreate its favorable terms.15

From Breakthrough to Rhythm

Haig and Foch initially considered renewing the attack on the same front. Air photographs, reports from the corps, and the stiffening defense persuaded them otherwise. Fourth Army would maintain pressure while Third Army attacked toward Bapaume and First Army prepared to move from the Arras sector. The decision surrendered the hope of an immediate thrust to Roye, but it also refused to spend experienced divisions against defenses that now expected them. The initiative passed from one battlefield to a sequence of battlefields.16

In that sequence, Amiens was both exceptional and instructive. No later attack reproduced its opening mass of tanks, and the Hundred Days did not become a continuous mechanized pursuit. Jonathan Boff's comparison of the Western Front in 1914 and 1918 instead points to a broader transformation: artillery had become more accurate and flexible, infantry carried far more automatic firepower, aircraft and motor transport expanded the commander's reach, and operations demanded more coordination across larger material systems. Tanks belonged to that transformation, but they did not replace its other parts.17

Allied armies consequently developed a method less dramatic than a single armored breakthrough. They prepared limited blows, shifted reserves and guns, attacked before the Germans could settle, and moved the main effort when resistance hardened. Each offensive forced German headquarters to spend troops, rail capacity, ammunition, and attention on another threatened sector. Amiens mattered because it showed that this rhythm could be imposed, even though the first attack itself had culminated.

Conclusion

The front moved at Amiens because Allied commanders joined advantages that had often appeared separately: a vulnerable salient, an exhausted enemy, concealed concentration, accurate artillery without warning fire, tanks, trained infantry, and fresh formations passing through successive objectives. Fog enlarged the surprise, especially in the center, while Canadian and Australian staffs converted it into depth. Where terrain, timing, or preparation differed, the results differed too.

Movement slowed when that arrangement lost its first conditions. German reserves arrived, surviving guns engaged the tanks, the ground and roads resisted the timetable, and supporting arms fell behind the infantry. Once those conditions vanished, Allied commanders shifted the campaign's weight to new ground instead of insisting that Amiens remain the battle it had been at 4:20 a.m. The attack opened no indefinite road east; its durable result was a German command compelled to answer the next blow before it had recovered from the last.
