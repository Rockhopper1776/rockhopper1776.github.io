The Army That Reached Adwa: Coalition, Supply, and Victory in 1896

When four Italian brigades advanced toward Adwa during the night of 29 February 1896, they expected to surprise an Ethiopian army nearing the limit of its supplies. Instead, they entered a series of separate fights against regional forces that could reinforce one another.

Introduction

Before dawn on 1 March 1896, Major General Matteo Albertone's brigade was moving through broken ground east of Adwa. It was supposed to form the Italian left while three other brigades occupied a connected line of hills. A faulty map and confusion over place names instead took Albertone farther forward, opening a gap between his men and the Italian center. Because Ethiopian scouts had already reported the advance, troops from Tigray, Gojjam, Wollo, Harar, and Menelik II's own force were moving toward the isolated column.1

The resulting battle is often explained through a contrast between a large Ethiopian army at home in mountainous country and a smaller European force lost in unfamiliar terrain. Both conditions mattered, but neither explains how an army drawn from distant regions assembled at Adwa, remained there through a supply crisis, or shifted men from one fight to the next. Terrain divided the Italian brigades only because their commanders chose a difficult night march and failed to maintain contact. Ethiopian superiority became decisive because political agreements, provisions, intelligence, and flexible command allowed regional armies to act together.

The coalition's achievement came with political and material limits. Menelik joined rulers with distinct interests, and the food that sustained their troops came at a cost to cultivators along the route. After the victory, the same problems of supply that had pressed the Italians toward battle helped prevent a deeper Ethiopian advance into Eritrea. The coalition could defeat Baratieri at Adwa; it could not indefinitely supply the same concentration north of the battlefield.

A Treaty Becomes a Colonial Claim

Italy and Menelik, then king of Shewa, signed the Treaty of Wuchale in May 1889. The agreement recognized Italian possessions north of the Mareb River and regulated trade and diplomacy, but its two official texts did not give Article 17 the same force. The Amharic version permitted Menelik to use the Italian government when dealing with foreign powers; the Italian version made that relationship appear obligatory. Italy used its text to claim a protectorate over Ethiopia. Menelik rejected the claim and, after efforts to resolve the difference failed, renounced the treaty.2

The dispute accompanied an Italian advance from the Red Sea colony of Eritrea into Tigray. Italian forces defeated Ras Mengesha Yohannes at Coatit and Senafe in early 1895, occupied Adwa and Mekelle, and built fortified positions along the route south. When Menelik issued a mobilization proclamation on 17 September, he was responding to an invasion already established on Ethiopian territory, not to an abstract disagreement over a document.

Nor did resistance to colonial expansion make Menelik's own state an equal union. During the years before Adwa, his armies had extended imperial rule across large parts of the south and southwest. Conquest, land grants, taxation, and the settlement of soldiers deprived many communities of earlier rights and made cultivators tenants on land controlled by the state, churches, or officials. The resources of this enlarged empire would help defeat Italy, but they had been assembled through both negotiation and coercion.3

An Army Made from a Coalition

Mobilization required Menelik to turn that uneven state into a temporary military coalition. Ras Mengesha, son of the former emperor Yohannes IV and ruler of Tigray, had his own claim to authority and had previously fought Menelik's rise. Yet the Italian occupation threatened his province first. Menelik reconciled with him and reached agreements with rulers in Gojjam, Begemder, Wag, Wollo, and other regions. These leaders agreed to bring troops, open their territories to supply, and accept an overall campaign directed by the emperor and a council of senior commanders.4

The coalition gathered armies with different regional identities and chains of command. Ras Makonnen brought men from Harar; King Tekle Haymanot led the Gojjam force; Ras Mikael commanded Wollo cavalry; Ras Mengesha and Ras Alula led Tigrayan troops. Empress Taytu accompanied her own following and took part in councils with Menelik and the commanders. Forces also remained behind to guard Addis Ababa, western districts, and the eastern frontier. Concentration in the north did not mean abandoning every other danger.

Tsegaye Tegenu estimates that more than 100,000 soldiers assembled at several points within two months of the proclamation. Figures for the force eventually present near Adwa vary widely, but an army of at least 90,000 is plausible. Many carried modern rifles obtained from several European suppliers, although models, ammunition, and training were far from uniform; others still carried spears and swords. The achievement lay in more than the final number. Menelik and the regional rulers had transformed agreements among former rivals into a march that Italy's leaders had not believed Ethiopia could organize so quickly.5

Feeding the March North

The army's size rested on a system of land, taxation, personal obligation, and regional contribution. Tegenu distinguishes the older riste gult arrangements of the northern provinces from the more centralized gebbar maderiya system that supported Menelik's court and imperial troops. Land revenue could maintain soldiers, while governors supplied provincial forces and auxiliary laborers moved guns, tents, food, and other equipment. Thousands of horses, mules, donkeys, cattle, and sheep traveled with the columns.6

Provisioning was distributed rather than managed by one commissariat. The court and governors prepared stores, local officials collected grain and honey, and ordinary soldiers were expected to begin with durable foods such as dried meat, flour, bread, and chickpeas. Servants gathered fuel, found forage, and prepared food. The campaign began after the rains, when grass and water were more readily available for the animals, and camps were chosen partly for access to both.

This arrangement made a long advance possible without railways or a single extended wagon line while leaving the army dependent on the country through which it moved. The route from central Ethiopia climbed and descended through broken ground for roughly a thousand kilometers. As the columns moved north, local productivity declined while the number of soldiers, followers, and animals increased. Stored provisions ran down; tribute, purchases, and foraging became more important; and long encampments exhausted nearby supplies. Some soldiers looted, and communities along the route bore losses that do not appear in the battle's order of battle.7

While the supply system carried the army north, the siege of Mekelle changed how Ethiopian commanders used force. Direct attacks against the fortified Italian position cost many lives. On Taytu's advice, Ethiopian troops occupied the spring that supplied the fort, and the garrison eventually negotiated its departure. The episode reinforced a practical lesson without providing a formula for Adwa: numbers and courage were most useful when they could be applied to an enemy's immediate weakness rather than spent against prepared fire.8

Two Armies Wait

By February, the Ethiopian army had moved beyond Mekelle and camped near Adwa while General Oreste Baratieri held a fortified position around Sauria. Each commander wanted the other to attack: Baratieri expected the enormous Ethiopian camp to consume its food and disperse, while Menelik had little reason to advance into Italian defenses as long as his own position remained strong. When an Ethiopian reconnaissance toward Serae found too little food and water to support the army, Ras Mengesha and Ras Alula also advised against assaulting Sauria.9

As the February standoff continued, supply shortages increased pressure in both camps. Ethiopian formations ranged outward in search of food, while Baratieri's supply position deteriorated and Tigrayan forces interfered with communications to Eritrea. On 25 February, Italian prime minister Francesco Crispi sent a telegram accusing the general of conducting a wasting campaign without a basic plan. Although the message stopped short of ordering an attack, it made clear that Rome expected action to restore military and monarchical prestige.

Baratieri had about 17,700 troops available for the advance, including roughly 10,500 Italians and several thousand Eritrean askari serving in the colonial army. Reports placed Menelik's force at several times that number. Baratieri nevertheless believed a night movement could seize a defensible line of hills before dawn, place his guns well, and force the Ethiopians either to attack uphill or withdraw. At a council on 28 February, his four brigade commanders opposed retreat and favored action. Reports that Ethiopian troops had scattered to find food made the opportunity appear temporary.10

Baratieri's decision ended the contest of endurance. Supplies and political pressure narrowed his choices, yet they did not determine the route, the hour, or the spacing of his columns. He ordered the four brigades to march that night.

The Night March Separates

The Italian advance began at about 9:00 p.m. on 29 February. Albertone's brigade moved on the left, Giuseppe Arimondi's in the center, Vittorio Dabormida's on the right, and Giuseppe Ellena's in reserve. Baratieri intended them to occupy mutually supporting positions along the hills of Kidane Mehret, Belah, and Rebbi Arienni. Instead, narrow tracks, darkness, defective maps, and confused place names pulled the formation apart. Albertone went too far toward Enda Kidane Mehret, while Dabormida moved away on the opposite flank.11

The gaps resulted from the demands of Baratieri's plan rather than from the mountains alone. Four brigades had to keep contact while moving at night over ground their maps represented poorly, and the schedule left little room to repair a mistake before daylight. Once gaps opened, runners and staff officers could not provide Baratieri with a reliable picture of all three leading columns. Firepower that might have been formidable on a connected line was divided among separate positions.

The Ethiopian camp, by contrast, did not have to execute one detailed march timetable. Scouts and local informants warned Ras Mengesha and Ras Alula that the Italians were approaching. Regional armies were encamped in known sectors, with Menelik and Taytu holding a substantial reserve near the center. Senior commanders could move toward the firing while other forces protected the camp and watched the remaining approaches. Their arrangement combined an overall purpose with room for initiative once the direction of the attack became clear.12

Concentration at Adwa

Fighting began around dawn at Albertone's advanced position. Tigrayan troops under Ras Mengesha and Ras Alula engaged first, soon joined by forces from Wollo, Gojjam, Harar, and Menelik's own command. Italian artillery inflicted heavy losses, but Ethiopian riflemen and gunners used folds in the ground and the surrounding heights to work against the brigade's flanks and rear. The important advantage was not that every contingent fought in the same way. It was that several contingents reached the same fight before the Italian center could restore contact.13

Albertone's brigade included many Eritrean askari, whose discipline allowed it to resist for hours. By late morning, however, its ammunition and cohesion were failing, Ethiopian forces had taken commanding ground, and Albertone surrendered. Survivors retreating toward the center collided with troops trying to reinforce or hold the line. What had begun as an isolated brigade action now carried disorder into Arimondi's position and Ellena's reserve.

Men from Harar, Tigray, Wollo, Gondar, Lasta, Gojjam, and the imperial force pressed the center while other groups moved to block the routes between the Italian brigades. Arimondi could not reestablish a continuous front, and Baratieri could not bring the reserve into the battle as an intact body because parts of it were already fighting for their own positions. Around noon, the center and reserve broke. The sequence mattered: troops no longer needed against Albertone could turn toward Arimondi and Ellena, increasing the force applied at the next point.14

Dabormida's brigade remained separated on the Italian right and did not receive a clear account of the collapse elsewhere. It fought through the afternoon against Tigrayan troops and cavalry from other regions, then faced men and guns released from the defeated Italian center. Dabormida was killed during the attempted withdrawal, and his brigade disintegrated. By about 4:00 p.m., the three leading Italian columns had been defeated in turn. Ethiopian command operated through regional leaders and an imperial reserve rather than a European-style staff, yet the coalition achieved concentration where Baratieri's more formally organized force had lost it.15

What Victory Could and Could Not Do

The destruction of the Italian columns produced severe losses whose exact totals remain uncertain. A contemporary British handbook placed deaths in the Italian-led force at 6,133 and reported 1,428 wounded and 1,865 Italian prisoners, while Ethiopian estimates range from several thousand to about 7,000 dead with many more wounded. Categories and totals differ among accounts, so they should be read as evidence of the battle's scale rather than a final ledger. The aftermath also resists a simple story of liberation. Menelik commuted death sentences imposed on 406 captured askari whom the Ethiopian court treated as traitors, but ordered the amputation of a hand and foot. The punishment exposed the violence of a war in which Eritreans had been recruited into Italy's colonial army and then judged by the state they had fought.16

Menelik's commanders considered moving farther north, and troops encircled the remaining Italian garrison at Adigrat. An advance into Eritrea, however, would have required food and water for tens of thousands of men and animals in a region already stripped by the campaign. The army had marched for months; soldiers wanted to return home; Italy was sending reinforcements; and other frontiers still demanded attention. Menelik abandoned the deeper advance and began moving south on 20 March. 17

Ending the advance short of Eritrea did not surrender the battle's central political result. Crispi's government fell, and British reporting that had treated Ethiopia as a lesser opponent rapidly reconsidered its assumptions after the scale of the defeat became known. On 26 October, the Treaty of Addis Ababa annulled Wuchale and recorded Italy's recognition of Ethiopia as an independent sovereign state. Italy nevertheless retained Eritrea. Ethiopian victory ended the protectorate claim and checked the invasion, but it did not restore every territory beyond the Mareb.18

Conclusion

Menelik and Taytu did not bring a uniform national army to Adwa. They helped assemble a coalition whose rulers supplied their own contingents, whose soldiers carried varied weapons, and whose food came from overlapping systems of taxation, obligation, storage, purchase, and foraging. Ras Mengesha and Ras Alula brought knowledge of Tigray and the earlier war; Ras Makonnen, Tekle Haymanot, Ras Mikael, and other commanders brought forces from distant regions. Their cooperation survived long enough to deny Baratieri the defensive battle he wanted.

When the Italian night march separated, that coalition turned preparation into battlefield action. Regional armies reached Albertone first, shifted toward the center, and then reinforced the fight against Dabormida. The food crisis that made such concentration temporary also stopped the campaign short of Eritrea. Seven months later, Italian and Ethiopian representatives met in Addis Ababa and signed a treaty recognizing the sovereignty that Baratieri's advance had been intended to remove.
