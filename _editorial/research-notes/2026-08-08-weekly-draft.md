## Selected subject

Proposed title: **Designed for the Unexpected: Command and Contingency at Cowpens**

- Era and date range: American Revolution, with the immediate setting in the southern campaign of 1780-1781 and the battle on January 17, 1781.
- Military domain: land warfare.
- Article type: focused battle and command study.
- Target length: 2,200-2,600 prose words.
- Central question: How did Daniel Morgan turn a mixed force of Continental infantry, militia, riflemen, and cavalry into a coherent defense at Cowpens, and how much of the result followed his design rather than battlefield contingency?
- Provisional thesis: Morgan's principal achievement was not the advance planning of a perfect double envelopment. He matched limited tasks to different bodies of troops, protected a dependable Continental core with depth and a cavalry reserve, and chose ground on which the parts could support one another. Tarleton's exhausting pursuit and rapid attack, a misunderstood order in John Eager Howard's line, and quick American adaptation transformed that resilient design into a far more complete victory than Morgan could have scripted.

This subject adds a focused American Revolutionary land battle and a command-and-contingency argument to a Writings collection presently weighted toward naval history, systems, and logistics. It does not duplicate any existing article.

### Scholarly and official secondary works

1. John Moncure, *The Cowpens Staff Ride and Battlefield Tour* (Fort Leavenworth, KS: Combat Studies Institute, U.S. Army Command and General Staff College, 1996). Official 208-page research and staff-ride monograph with a documented battle analysis, maps, order of battle, and extensive transcriptions of American and British accounts. Principal analytical source, especially pp. 43-70.
2. Edwin C. Bearss, *The Battle of Cowpens: A Documented Narrative and Troop Movement Maps* (Washington, DC: Office of Archeology and Historic Preservation, National Park Service, 1967). Official documented narrative with source notes and phase maps. Principal corroborating secondary source, especially pp. 6-28.
3. Thomas J. Fleming, *Cowpens: "Downright Fighting"* (Washington, DC: National Park Service, 1988). Official National Park Handbook 135. Used cautiously for narrative orientation and craft study, not as the sole authority for contested details.
4. National Park Service, Cowpens National Battlefield, "The Battle of Cowpens" and "Forty-eight Hours Following the Battle of Cowpens." Current institutional syntheses used to check battlefield setting, campaign sequence, and the immediate movement of prisoners and survivors.

### Primary and participant sources

5. Daniel Morgan to Nathanael Greene, January 19, 1781, after-action report, reprinted in Moncure, pp. 122-24. Written two days after the battle; principal near-contemporary American command account. Morgan described the deployment, ordered militia withdrawals, Continental counterattack, captures, and his own claimed strengths and losses.
6. John Eager Howard, later accounts written to correct published narratives, reprinted in Moncure, pp. 126-31. Principal American account for the misunderstood attempt to refuse the right flank, the orderly withdrawal, the re-formation, and Howard's bayonet charge. Its later date requires caution.
7. Samuel Hammond, later reconstruction of Morgan's operations order and the battlefield, reprinted in Moncure, pp. 131-36. The only surviving detailed reconstruction of the order, reportedly based on notes Hammond made on the evening of January 16. Useful but not identical to Morgan's own report.
8. Banastre Tarleton, *A History of the Campaigns of 1780 and 1781 in the Southern Provinces of North America* (London: T. Cadell, 1787), especially pp. 214-21, with the battle passage reprinted in Moncure, pp. 148-51. Principal British command account; self-exculpatory and compared against Mackenzie.
9. Roderick Mackenzie, *Strictures on Lieutenant-Colonel Tarleton's History "of the Campaigns of 1780 and 1781, in the Southern Provinces of North America"* (London: R. Jameson, 1787), Cowpens discussion reprinted in Moncure, pp. 151-56. A hostile counter-account by a 71st Highlanders officer who was present; valuable precisely because it disputes Tarleton, but also shaped by personal controversy.

## Interpretive cautions and contested points

1. **The three-line diagram is an analytical reconstruction.** Morgan, Hammond, Howard, and later witnesses disagree on precise locations, unit groupings, and the geometry of the militia positions. The article will explain the functional sequence without pretending that every yard is certain.
2. **The Broad River was not directly at the army's back.** It was several miles away. Later claims that Morgan simply trapped his troops against the river compress geography and give one retrospective explanation too much weight.
3. **The exact number of American troops is disputed.** Morgan reported only 800 engaged, two-thirds militia. Later reconstructions count militia who arrived late or were omitted from the formal return and produce substantially higher totals. The article will avoid a false precise total and distinguish contemporary claims from later estimates.
4. **The "two fires and retire" formula is less secure than popular retellings imply.** Morgan's immediate report describes regimented fire followed by withdrawal according to orders but does not prescribe an identical number of volleys for every militia element. Bearss and later witnesses support a limited-fire withdrawal. The article will describe the sequence without making the number two carry more evidentiary weight than it can bear.
5. **The double envelopment was probably not preplanned.** Moncure concludes that evidence does not support a scripted Hannibal-like maneuver. The enveloping shape emerged when militia and cavalry returned as Howard's line counterattacked.
6. **Tarleton was aggressive, not simply irrational.** His pursuit sought to prevent Morgan from gathering reinforcements or crossing the Broad, and open ground seemed favorable to British regulars and cavalry. His consequential error was pressing an exhausted, partly undeployed force into attack and then losing the ability to control reserves and pursuit.
7. **Cowpens did not mechanically cause Yorktown.** It destroyed a valuable British light corps, lifted Patriot confidence, forced Cornwallis into pursuit, and strengthened the sequence that led through the race to the Dan and Guilford Courthouse. Other decisions, battles, logistics, French intervention, and the Virginia campaign remained necessary.

## Outline

1. **Introduction:** Begin with the familiar clean diagram of three American lines and a closing double envelopment, then expose the battlefield uncertainty that the diagram conceals. State the command-and-contingency thesis.
2. **An Army That Could Not Absorb Another Camden:** Explain the southern campaign, Greene's divided army, Morgan's mission, Tarleton's pursuit, and why Morgan had to balance survival with the political cost of retreat.
3. **Ground for a Mixed Army:** Introduce the Cowpens as open grazing ground, correct the river-at-the-back simplification, and explain how Morgan used terrain and intelligence.
4. **A Plan in Layers:** Follow the functional sequence from skirmishers through militia and Continentals to Washington's cavalry reserve. Emphasize task design and uncertainty about exact geometry.
5. **Tarleton Sees a Rout:** Reconstruct the British night march, deployment, assault, militia withdrawal, and Tarleton's decision to press.
6. **The Retreat That Was Not the Plan:** Explain Howard's attempt to refuse the flank, the misunderstood order, the disciplined re-formation, countervolley, bayonet charge, cavalry response, and militia return.
7. **A Victory Larger Than the Design:** Assess losses, captures, campaign consequences, the limits of a direct Cowpens-to-Yorktown story, and why flexibility is a more persuasive measure of Morgan's command than perfect choreography.
8. **Conclusion:** Return to the difference between a robust plan and a scripted outcome.

## Private craft notes

- The strongest official narratives move from a concrete tactical problem to an analytical question rather than opening with a catalog of units. Adopt that reader orientation while using independent language and structure.
- Moncure often tests a celebrated story against conflicting witnesses before offering a bounded conclusion. Use the general pattern of evidence, disagreement, and qualified inference, but do not reproduce his phrasing or sequence.
- Bearss makes terrain legible by connecting each physical feature to a command choice. Introduce the Cowpens before assigning it tactical roles, and keep distances selective rather than overwhelming the reader.
- Fleming sustains momentum by alternating pursuit, preparation, and character. The article can borrow that broad rhythm while avoiding his novelistic scene-setting, reconstructed dialogue, and confident access to thoughts.
- Paragraphs should carry consequences forward: a militia withdrawal matters because it pulled British infantry toward Howard's line; the misunderstood order matters because discipline preserved a retreat that the British misread.
- Use no reflexive "this matters" sentences. Keep em dashes out unless no clearer punctuation serves.

- Checked the British march and deployment against Tarleton and Mackenzie. Their conflict is represented explicitly, and neither participant is treated as impartial.
- Checked the Continental withdrawal and counterattack against Howard, Bearss, and Moncure. The draft identifies the withdrawal as unplanned but orderly and attributes the subsequent decisions to Howard and Morgan.
- Checked the cavalry and returning-militia sequence against Bearss, Moncure, and the collected participant accounts. The draft describes an enveloping result without claiming a fully preplanned double envelopment.
- Checked loss and prisoner claims against Morgan, Tarleton, and Moncure. The figures are identified as Morgan's approximate report, followed immediately by the British disagreement and the broader strength problem.
- Checked immediate consequences against Bearss, Moncure, and the National Park Service's "Forty-eight Hours" account. The draft rejects a direct or inevitable Cowpens-to-Yorktown chain.
- Verified all 16 endnotes against the cited passages and confirmed that every bibliography entry is used in the notes. No discovered-but-unused work appears in the bibliography.

Remaining evidentiary limitations:

- Precise American strength cannot be recovered confidently. Morgan's report of 800 engaged conflicts with later reconstructions that include additional militia.
- Exact company positions and the number of militia volleys vary among accounts.
- Hammond and Howard wrote their fullest accounts after the battle; each is used with date and purpose in mind.
- Mackenzie's testimony helps expose weaknesses in Tarleton's self-defense, but their published controversy makes Mackenzie an interested witness.
- No surviving order proves that Morgan planned the final double envelopment. The draft follows Moncure's bounded conclusion that the plan created flexibility and the enveloping finish emerged during the battle.
