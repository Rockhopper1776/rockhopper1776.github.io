## Selection and scope

- Proposed title: *Supplying the Left Hook: Logistics and Operation Desert Storm*
- Era and date range: Persian Gulf crisis and war, August 2, 1990-February 28, 1991
- Domain and type: Land warfare; operational logistics and campaign analysis
- Central question: How did coalition logistics make the western envelopment in Operation Desert Storm possible, and what limits were concealed by the campaign's speed?
- Provisional thesis: The western envelopment was enabled by strategic airlift and sealift, prepositioned stocks, Saudi infrastructure and contracted support, coalition transport, centralized theater logistics, and the decision to delay the westward buildup until the air campaign protected the deception. The achievement was substantial but contingent: months without Iraqi interference allowed the system to overcome poor visibility, lift delays, transport shortages, and a supply network that was nearing its forward limits when the cease-fire arrived.
- Target: 2,200-2,800 prose words; a focused campaign and operational-system study.

The subject does not duplicate the existing naval studies of frigates and Quiberon Bay or the air-defense study of the Dowding System. It adds a post-1945 land-warfare article and an operational-logistics article to the site's current mix.

## Source audit

Eight substantive sources were consulted. The core evidence base includes two independent analytical works, an official multi-author history, a participant-authored professional study, and three contemporaneous GAO audits.

1. Stephen Biddle, “Victory Misunderstood: What the Gulf War Tells Us about the Future of Conflict,” *International Security* 21, no. 2 (1996): 139-79. Scholarly secondary analysis; used to prevent logistics from becoming a single-cause explanation of battlefield success.
2. Richard M. Swain, *“Lucky War”: Third Army in Desert Storm* (1994). Independent official analytical history based on command records and interviews; principal source for planning, transport requirements, and the forward buildup.
3. Frank N. Schubert and Theresa L. Kraus, eds., *The Whirlwind War* (1995). Official multi-author history; principal source for the westward movement, forward bases, host-nation transport, and battlefield sustainment.
4. William G. Pagonis and Michael D. Krause, *Operational Logistics and the Gulf War* (1992). Participant-authored professional analysis; used with attribution and checked against GAO and independent histories.
5. U.S. General Accounting Office, *Operation Desert Storm: Transportation and Distribution of Equipment and Supplies in Southwest Asia*, GAO/NSIAD-92-20 (1991). Contemporaneous audit; principal critical source on supply visibility, surface transport, ports, and host-nation dependence.
6. U.S. General Accounting Office, *Desert Shield/Storm: U.S. Transportation Command's Support of Operation*, GAO/NSIAD-92-54 (1992). Contemporaneous audit; source for strategic-lift totals, delays, planning problems, and favorable conditions.
7. U.S. General Accounting Office, *Desert Shield/Storm: Air Mobility Command's Achievements and Lessons for the Future*, GAO/NSIAD-93-40 (1993). Contemporaneous audit; source for strategic airlift totals, Civil Reserve Air Fleet participation, and port constraints.
8. Samuel J. Cox, “H-061-3: Desert Storm-Sealift, Seabees, Navy Medicine” (2021). Official Naval History and Heritage Command overview; used as a cross-check on strategic sealift and prepositioning.

All cited works were opened and inspected. PDF page images were checked for representative statistical, map, and table pages in addition to text extraction. Bibliographic details and stable links were verified from the issuing institutions or journal metadata.

## Evidence, interpretation, and uncertainty

Established evidence:

- Strategic lift moved nearly all personnel by air and the overwhelming majority of heavy cargo by sea.
- The first defensive deployment deliberately prioritized combat forces over support forces; prepositioned stocks and Saudi support bought time while the theater logistics structure formed.
- The western envelopment required both corps and their supply system to shift hundreds of miles while forward bases were created after the air campaign began.
- Heavy-equipment transporters, line-haul trucks, drivers, and material-handling equipment remained scarce; contracted, donated, and host-nation assets were essential.
- Supply visibility was poor during the initial “push,” and transport planning and information systems did not perform as intended.
- During the advance, leading formations at times outpaced fuel distribution. The official history judged that a longer ground war would have required an operational pause to refill forward bases.

Interpretive judgment:

The article treats logistics as a necessary condition for the left hook, not a sufficient explanation for the coalition's one-sided tactical victories. Biddle's argument about the interaction of skill and technology is represented to keep the causal claim proportionate. Pagonis and Krause's favorable assessment of centralized logistics is presented as a participant interpretation and triangulated against the GAO audits' evidence of friction.

Weakest evidentiary point:

The precise degree to which logistical timing lengthened the air campaign is difficult to isolate from air-campaign objectives and political decisions. Pagonis and Krause state that the shift from a two-week to a three-week logistics timeline contributed to the extension, but the draft does not claim logistics alone determined the date of the ground offensive.

Source limitations and uncertainty:

- Aggregate totals vary with cutoff date, service, category, and whether fuel is counted as cargo. Each quantitative claim in the draft is tied to a named source and date rather than blended into a single grand total.
- GAO/NSIAD-92-20 did not inspect distribution at the forward bases because major units were already redeploying during its field visit. Its findings are strongest for ports, storage, and theater surface transport and are supplemented by Swain and *The Whirlwind War* for forward operations.
- Participant accounts naturally emphasize command achievements. They are useful for intent and planning but are not allowed to stand alone on disputed performance claims.
- The campaign's brevity creates a counterfactual problem: sources agree that a longer advance would have required a pause, but the exact day or location of that pause cannot be established confidently.

## Private craft notes

- Strong operational history becomes readable when it begins with a physical problem: distance, weight, time, and the sequence in which people and equipment must arrive.
- Numbers work best when attached to a decision or bottleneck. A truck count should explain why a plan could or could not move, not serve as spectacle.
- The most useful narrative path follows the supply chain: home port, ocean crossing, Saudi reception, western movement, forward base, combat formation.
- Participant voices add human scale, but the article should introduce the speaker's role and preserve analytical distance.
- A warm academic voice can acknowledge ingenuity without turning the story into institutional celebration; pairing achievement with the friction that made it necessary keeps the reader oriented.
