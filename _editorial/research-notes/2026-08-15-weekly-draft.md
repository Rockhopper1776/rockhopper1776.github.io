### Selected subject

Proposed title: **Between the Springs and the Lake: Saladin and the Battle of Hattin**

- Era and date range: the Crusades in the late twelfth century, with operational context from Saladin's earlier campaigns and a principal focus on July 2-4, 1187.
- Military domain: land warfare.
- Article type: focused battle, operational command, and combined-arms study.
- Target length: 2,400-2,800 prose words.
- Central question: How did Saladin turn the Frankish march from the Springs of Saffuriya into a decisive defeat at Hattin, and how confidently can the roles of water, command mistrust, terrain, and horse-foot coordination be reconstructed from conflicting sources?
- Provisional thesis: Saladin did not win merely because the Franks were thirsty or divided. By attacking Tiberias he forced a politically difficult operational choice; once the Frankish field army left its well-watered position, his forces contested its movement, controlled access to reliable water, sustained encirclement and missile pressure, and exploited the progressive separation of infantry and cavalry. The broad mechanism is recoverable, but the surviving Latin and Muslim accounts do not support a confident minute-by-minute sequence.
- Editorial rationale: Hattin adds the site's first medieval and Middle Eastern-centered article, a new geography and political setting, and a source-critical battle study. Although it is a land subject after two recent land articles, it provides more chronological and cultural variety than a third naval article. Tsushima and Alesia both passed their source-access tests and remain viable future topics; neither was rejected for inadequate evidence.

### Evidence map

- Saladin's need to force a decision after earlier campaigns failed to produce a decisive battle: Kedar, 190-93.
- Muslim concentration, approach through Kafr Sabt, road control, and the attack on Tiberias: Kedar, 193-95; Ibn al-Athir, 119-20; Baha al-Din, 110-11.
- Frankish concentration at the Springs of Saffuriya and the political-command dispute: *Libellus* in Brundage, 153–55; Ibn al-Athir, 119–21; Edbury, Lyon para. 40, 45, and Archumbald doc. 4b, 160. Reported speeches are treated as later narrative constructions, not transcripts.
- Departure from Saffuriya and harassment of the fighting march: Edbury, Lyon para. 40, 45, Colbert-Fontainebleau doc. 4a, 158–60, and Archumbald doc. 4b, 160–62; *Libellus* in Brundage, 156–58; Kedar, 195–99.
- Water as an access problem rather than a single bad choice: Kedar, 195-99. Kedar's spring measurements indicate that Ayn Turan could not sustain thousands of people and animals, so leaving it was not automatically a blunder. Reliable water remained at Saffuriya, the Hattin springs, and the lake, but Muslim movement increasingly denied or contested the approaches.
- The halt near Maskana, the overnight encirclement, and unequal resupply: Kedar, 197–202; Ibn al-Athir, 121–22; Edbury, Lyon paras. 40–41, 45–46, Colbert-Fontainebleau doc. 4a, 158–60, and Archumbald doc. 4b, 160–62. Exact location and the army's intended next destination remain disputed.
- Functional dependence of Frankish infantry and cavalry: Bennett, abstract; *Libellus* in Brundage, 157–58; Ibn al-Athir, 121–23. Infantry protected horses and mounted troops from missiles; mounted troops in turn gave the foot soldiers striking power and protection from charges.
- Fire, smoke, arrows, heat, and horse losses: Kedar, 202–4; Edbury, Lyon paras. 41–42, 45–47, Colbert-Fontainebleau doc. 4a, 158–60, and Archumbald doc. 4b, 160–62; Ibn al-Athir, 121–22. Sources disagree on who set the fire, when it burned, and where it fits in the sequence; it is treated as one pressure among several.
- Terrain and restricted mounted movement near the Horns: Lewis, Avissar Lewis, and Finkel, especially 41-45; Kedar, 204-06. The archaeology supports constraint, not proof of a fully preselected trap.
- Separation, repeated charges, Raymond's escape, the ascent to the Horns, capture of the True Cross, and collapse: Kedar, 202–6; Edbury, Lyon paras. 42–43, 46–48, Colbert-Fontainebleau doc. 4a, 158–60, and Archumbald doc. 4b, 160–62; *Libellus* in Brundage, 157–59; Ibn al-Athir, 122–24. Only the broad process is asserted because episode order and motives conflict.
- Immediate outcome and consequences: Ibn al-Athir, 123–25; Baha al-Din, 113–19; Edbury, 1–2; Prawer, 670–80. The field army and much of its leadership were killed or captured, opening much of the kingdom to Saladin's subsequent campaign, but Tyre held and Frankish power did not disappear in a single day.

### Interpretive cautions

1. **Thirst was produced and exploited, not self-explanatory.** July heat and water shortage harmed the Franks, but the article must show how Tiberias, route control, harassment, the halt, and encirclement made those conditions decisive.
2. **Guy's choice was risky, not incomprehensible.** Tiberias carried political and military weight, and the army could plausibly hope to reach dependable water while relieving the city. Later blame narratives simplify the council and assign invented speeches.
3. **The exact tactical sequence cannot be recovered.** Sources disagree on the halt, fire, Raymond's movement, the infantry's ascent, successive charges, and the capture of the True Cross. The article will reconstruct a causal mechanism rather than choreography.
4. **Army totals are unstable and rhetorical.** No precise strength, casualty, or prisoner figure will be presented as established fact.
5. **Landscape constrained action without determining it.** Field walls, rock piles, routes, and slopes limited mounted movement; the evidence does not show that Saladin knew or planned every local effect in advance.
6. **Coalition mistrust was a vulnerability, not a complete explanation.** Frankish arms had previously endured under pressure. Mistrust narrowed options and coordination, while sustained Muslim action converted strain into collapse.
7. **Muslim victory accounts are not transparent command records.** Ibn al-Athir and Baha al-Din preserve valuable operational details but also speeches, providential claims, and triumphal framing.
8. **Hattin was immediately catastrophic but not the automatic end of the crusader states.** Saladin's later conquests and the fall of Jerusalem followed from the destruction of the field army, while Tyre's survival and the Third Crusade show the limits of a one-battle endpoint.

## Concrete source craft notes

Record adaptable techniques only, never distinctive wording, syntax, voice, or structure.

### Word and clause level

- Kedar places qualifications next to the claim they limit, using calibrated terms for probability and explicit source attribution before inference. Adapt the technique by writing "probably" or "the accounts suggest" in the clause where uncertainty begins, not as a vague disclaimer after a confident paragraph.
- Bennett repeatedly uses one functional term, coordination, to bind infantry, horses, missile defense, and mounted shock into a relationship. Purposeful repetition of a key noun can make the mechanism legible without repeating an entire transition formula.
- Lewis, Avissar Lewis, and Finkel distinguish what the archaeology demonstrates from what they suggest about battle effects. Keep verbs of evidence separate: sources "report," material features "constrain," and the article "infers."
- Avoid translating medieval confessional labels into the narrator's voice. Use "Franks," "Frankish army," "Muslim forces," and "Saladin's army" descriptively; reserve hostile terms from the sources for source criticism rather than quotation.

### Sentence level

- Kedar often joins evidence and assessment in one sentence: a source reports a movement, while terrain or hydrology sets the boundary on what that report can mean. Use the same general relationship so that fact and interpretation remain adjacent without copying his syntax.
- Bennett defines a military relationship before describing its failure. Sentences should first explain what infantry did for mounted troops and what cavalry did for infantry, then show how thirst, casualties, and movement pulled those functions apart.
- The strongest source comparisons use contrast to advance knowledge rather than merely list disagreement. Pair "one account places" with "another moves" and then state the narrower conclusion both can support.
- Reserve short sentences for a genuine turn after a developed causal sequence. Do not stack isolated declarations such as "The army halted. The water was gone. The ring closed."

### Paragraph level

- Kedar's reconstruction repeatedly begins from a problem raised by the preceding evidence, tests a familiar explanation, and ends with a bounded conclusion that creates the next question. Each article paragraph should likewise open by carrying forward a decision, resource, or formation from the paragraph before it.
- Bennett's comparative method establishes that the fighting march could work, including under Guy in 1183, before turning to Hattin. This prevents outcome knowledge from making failure appear inevitable and gives the breakdown paragraph an analytical baseline.
- Lewis and colleagues begin with visible landscape features, revise the first explanation for them, and connect their older material history to a later tactical constraint. Adapt the evidence-to-reinterpretation movement, but do not reproduce their deep-prehistory structure in miniature.
- Primary narratives often move through vivid sensations and blame. Use a small number of concrete details such as smoke, arrows, exhausted horses, or inaccessible water only after the paragraph has established whose account supplies them and what analytical work they perform.

### Section level

- Kedar follows a chronological spine but pauses where roads, springs, and conflicting sources require analysis. The article will preserve that balance: decision at Tiberias, movement from Saffuriya, containment near Maskana, collapse near the Horns, then an explicit source-limit audit.
- Section endings should convert one kind of pressure into the next: political choice becomes movement; movement becomes a water problem; the water problem becomes containment; containment tests combined-arms cohesion; collapse exposes the limits of narrative reconstruction.
- The uncertainty section comes after the broad mechanism has been established. This lets it refine what readers know rather than interrupt every event with an undifferentiated catalogue of doubts.

### Whole-idea level

- Kedar's hydrological evidence overturns the inherited claim that leaving Ayn Turan was obviously foolish. The larger adaptable lesson is to test a famous mistake story against the material capacity of the supposedly missed option.
- Bennett reframes crusader tactics as cooperation between arms rather than as the independent charge of armored knights. The article's causal center should be a system losing its relationships, not a morality tale about one arm or one commander.
- Lewis and colleagues treat the battlefield as a layered landscape whose older features shaped later possibilities. Use terrain as a set of affordances and constraints, not as a deterministic actor.
- The article's learning path will move from a familiar sensory explanation, thirst and smoke, to the operational chain that made those pressures cumulative. It will end by distinguishing a recoverable mechanism from an unrecoverable sequence.

## Handoff-aware outline

1. **Introduction: Beyond Thirst**
   - Begin with the remembered images of heat, smoke, arrows, and the Horns, then establish that none alone explains the battle.
   - State the thesis and the source limitation: reconstruct the mechanism, not a minute-by-minute diagram.
   - Handoff: ask how Saladin brought those pressures together, leading backward from the battlefield to Tiberias.
2. **Tiberias as a Lever**
   - Establish the Frankish field army at the well-watered Springs of Saffuriya and Saladin's difficulty forcing a decisive encounter there.
   - Explain the attack on Tiberias as a political-operational lever with uncertain outcome, not magical bait.
   - Compare the reported council arguments without treating speeches as transcripts or placing all blame on Guy, Raymond, or Reynald.
   - Handoff: the council's contested choice becomes a physical column leaving reliable water.
3. **The Road Becomes the Battlefield**
   - Introduce the fighting march and the interdependence of infantry, cavalry, horses, baggage, and missile defense before describing its strain.
   - Reframe water as access to a network of sources under pressure; explain why Ayn Turan was not a simple lost salvation.
   - Bring the march to its halt near Maskana as contested movement becomes containment.
   - Handoff: failure to reach dependable water gives Saladin the night and the space to tighten the ring.
4. **A Siege Without Walls**
   - Explain unequal sustainment: Muslim access to lake water and arrows against a surrounded force unable to rest or recover.
   - Treat fire, smoke, harassment, and terrain as interacting pressures, with explicit source cautions.
   - Handoff: those pressures become decisive only when the combined-arms formation can no longer keep its parts mutually supporting.
5. **When the Fighting March Failed**
   - Show how horse losses, infantry movement, restricted ground, local breakthroughs or escapes, and repeated charges progressively separated the army.
   - Describe the Horns as a defensible refuge and final position, not merely a place panicked fugitives happened to climb.
   - Stop short of a settled sequence for Raymond's escape, the True Cross, and the king's tent.
   - Handoff: the collapse is clear as a process even though its episodes appear in different orders.
6. **The Battle We Cannot Reassemble**
   - Compare the Latin blame traditions and Muslim victory narratives.
   - Identify what remains disputed and what can still be concluded about decision, water access, pressure, and cohesion.
   - Handoff: once the claim is bounded, the article can assess the victory's immediate scale without inflating its long-term inevitability.
7. **A Kingdom Without Its Field Army**
   - Describe the destruction or capture of much of the field army and leadership, the loss of the True Cross, Saladin's rapid follow-on conquests, and Jerusalem's surrender.
   - Balance consequence with Tyre's survival and the Third Crusade.
   - Handoff: the scale of the outcome returns attention to the mechanism that made the field army vulnerable.
8. **Conclusion: Advantage Made Cumulative**
   - Return to thirst and division as effects within a chain rather than isolated causes.
   - Conclude that Saladin's achievement was the sustained conversion of political, geographic, logistical, and tactical pressures into mutual failure inside the Frankish formation.
