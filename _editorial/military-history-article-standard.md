# Rockhopper 1776 Military History Article Research and Editorial Standard

Version 1.0  
Established: August 3, 2026  
Status: Mandatory for all automated military-history article research and drafting

## Mandatory opening requirement

Before selecting a topic, conducting a search, opening a source, outlining an article, or drafting prose, the researcher must read this entire document.

The researcher must then state in its working notes that this standard was reviewed. If this document is missing, inaccessible, incomplete, or internally contradictory, the task must stop and report the problem. Research may not proceed from memory or from a summary of an earlier version.

This document governs research, historical method, writing, citation, website preparation, verification, and editorial handoff. A task-specific instruction from the site owner may refine these requirements, but may not silently weaken standards for accuracy, source verification, or citation integrity.

## Purpose

Rockhopper 1776 publishes accessible, engaging military history grounded in serious scholarship. Articles should be understandable to interested general readers while meeting the evidentiary and interpretive standards expected of academic historical writing.

The objective is not merely to summarize events. Each article should advance a clear thesis or organizing interpretation, explain why its subject matters, distinguish evidence from inference, and help readers understand the choices, constraints, institutions, technologies, and historical circumstances that shaped military events.

## Chronological and subject scope

Articles may address military history from the Bronze Age through the conclusion of Operation Desert Storm in 1991.

Permitted subjects include:

- leaders and command;
- tactics, doctrine, training, and military institutions;
- weapons, vehicles, ships, aircraft, fortifications, logistics, and technological development;
- battle narratives;
- campaigns and theaters of war;
- overviews of wars;
- intelligence, reconnaissance, communications, mobilization, supply, medicine, and other operational systems;
- the political, economic, geographic, social, and cultural conditions necessary to explain military events;
- land, naval, amphibious, and air warfare.

Post-1991 conflicts and contemporary military controversies are outside the current editorial scope. Later scholarship may be used to study earlier events, but the article itself must not drift into present-day operational advocacy, partisan commentary, or analysis of conflicts after Desert Storm unless the site owner explicitly expands the scope.

## Topic selection

Before selecting a topic, review all existing Writings cards and article pages. Avoid duplicating an existing article or producing a substantially similar treatment under a different title.

### Owner-directed weekly assignments

The site owner reserves the right to direct any week’s article. An owner-directed assignment takes priority over automatic topic selection.

The site owner may provide the assignment by returning to the Codex task used to manage this publication workflow and specifying any combination of:

- the required topic;
- a central question, argument, thesis, or interpretive approach;
- the desired article type, military domain, era, or geographic scope;
- a target length within the established 1,000–5,000-word range;
- required people, events, technologies, sources, or historiographical debates;
- subjects, claims, sources, comparisons, or approaches to exclude;
- special organizational, stylistic, or website requirements;
- any other instructions that apply to that week’s article.

When the site owner provides a weekly assignment, Codex must record it as a dated directive in a durable location available to the scheduled task. At the beginning of the scheduled run, immediately after reading this standard and before any automatic topic selection, the researcher must check for an active owner directive for that week.

If an active directive exists, the researcher must follow it and must not substitute a different topic merely because another subject appears easier or more attractive. Requirements not addressed by the directive continue to be governed by this standard. The directive may refine scope and method, but it does not silently waive requirements for accuracy, source verification, Chicago citations, disclosure of conjecture, fact-checking, or editorial review.

If a directive conflicts with this standard, falls outside the current chronological scope, or cannot be supported by adequate sources, the task must stop and alert the site owner rather than reinterpret or disregard the instruction. The site owner may then revise the assignment or explicitly amend the governing standard.

A weekly directive applies only to the dated publication cycle for which it was issued. It must not be carried into a later week or treated as a permanent editorial rule unless the site owner explicitly says so. After the task uses a directive, the review report must confirm that the owner-selected topic and special requirements were followed.

Topic selection should maintain variety over time. Consider the recent publication history and avoid repeatedly selecting the same:

- historical era;
- nation or culture;
- war or campaign;
- military domain;
- article type;
- individual commander;
- interpretive theme.

The overall body of work should balance land, naval, and air subjects and should range across leaders, tactics, technologies, battles, wars, weapons, and campaigns. Balance is a long-term editorial objective, not a mechanical quota for any single article.

Prefer subjects for which sufficient reliable scholarship is accessible. A compelling topic with inadequate verifiable sources is not ready for publication.

## Article length

Articles must contain between 1,000 and 5,000 words, excluding endnotes and bibliography. Length should follow the needs of the subject rather than a fixed weekly target.

- 1,000–1,500 words: a narrowly defined weapon, episode, tactical problem, or biographical decision.
- 1,500–2,500 words: a standard battle narrative, leader study, technological development, or focused campaign question.
- 2,500–3,500 words: a substantial campaign, doctrinal development, comparative analysis, or complex battle.
- 3,500–5,000 words: a war overview, major campaign, or broad subject requiring multiple theaters, phases, or interpretations.

Do not pad an article to reach a longer category. Do not compress a complex subject so severely that essential context, disagreement, or evidence disappears. If the subject cannot be treated responsibly within 5,000 words, narrow the thesis or divide it into a clearly planned series.

## Source standards

### Source hierarchy

Research should prioritize sources in approximately this order:

1. Relevant primary sources, including official records, correspondence, diaries, memoirs used critically, contemporary reports, orders, maps, and material evidence.
2. Peer-reviewed journal articles and scholarly monographs from university or established academic presses.
3. Official military histories and research published by reputable government historical offices.
4. Critical editions, documentary collections, and reference works produced by recognized scholars or institutions.
5. Publications from reputable archives, museums, libraries, universities, and professional historical organizations.
6. High-quality specialist works from established trade presses when supported by scholarly apparatus and a strong professional reputation.

General reference sites, popular summaries, search snippets, unsourced webpages, videos, forums, social media, and commercial listicles may help identify terminology or leads, but they are not adequate authorities for important claims. Wikipedia may be used only for preliminary orientation and source discovery; it must never appear in the article's endnotes or bibliography.

AI-generated text, AI summaries, and citations suggested by an AI system are not sources. Every cited work must be independently located and verified.

### Minimum research depth

Use enough sources to represent the subject responsibly. As a baseline:

- 1,000–1,500 words: at least four substantive sources.
- 1,500–3,000 words: at least six substantive sources.
- 3,000–5,000 words: at least eight substantive sources.

At least two sources should normally be scholarly secondary works. Whenever appropriate and accessible, include at least one primary source or official documentary collection. Source counts are minimum safeguards, not substitutes for source quality or intellectual diversity.

Do not manufacture variety by citing several derivative works that repeat the same account. For disputed or consequential claims, seek genuinely independent evidence or contrasting scholarly interpretations.

### Source verification

Before citing a source, verify as much of the following as applicable:

- author or responsible institution;
- complete title;
- editor or translator;
- publication or journal title;
- publisher;
- publication date;
- volume, issue, and page range;
- edition;
- DOI, archive identifier, or stable URL;
- the specific page or section supporting the claim.

Never invent a source, quotation, page number, DOI, archive reference, publication detail, or URL. Never cite a work merely because another author cites it unless the cited material has been consulted directly. If exact bibliographic information or supporting text cannot be verified, omit that citation, locate another source, or stop and disclose the limitation.

Search-result excerpts and AI-generated summaries do not establish that a source supports a claim. Open and inspect the source itself whenever access permits. Do not imply that a paywalled or inaccessible work was consulted in full.

### Corroboration

Corroborate major factual claims, disputed events, casualty figures, quotations, motives, and claims of causation whenever the source record allows. When reliable accounts conflict, do not force false certainty. Explain the disagreement, identify the evidentiary problem, and describe which interpretation appears strongest and why.

Quantitative claims require particular care. State whether figures are contemporary estimates, later reconstructions, official totals, or contested approximations. Use qualified language where precision is not justified.

## Historical method and interpretation

### Fact, inference, and conjecture

Clearly distinguish among:

- facts directly established by the available evidence;
- interpretations advanced by identified historians;
- reasonable inferences drawn from the evidence;
- conjectures offered to explore unresolved questions.

Assumptions and conjectures are permitted when historical ambiguity warrants them, but they must be academically supportable and clearly identified. Use language such as “probably,” “likely,” “may have,” “the available evidence suggests,” or “one plausible interpretation is.” Explain the evidence and reasoning behind the inference, cite the scholarship that makes it plausible, and acknowledge meaningful alternatives.

Never present speculation as a discovered fact. Never assign a private motive to a historical actor without evidence. Avoid treating an outcome as inevitable merely because it is known in hindsight.

### Competing interpretations

Where historians disagree materially, represent the strongest relevant interpretations fairly. Do not create artificial balance between a well-supported scholarly consensus and an unsupported fringe claim. Explain why interpretations differ, including variations in source access, methodology, national tradition, chronology, or definitions.

The article may reach a reasoned conclusion, but it should show readers where the evidence ends and interpretation begins.

### Context and anachronism

Judge historical actors and institutions within the constraints, knowledge, language, and assumptions of their time. Avoid presentism, technological determinism, national mythology, hero worship, and moral simplification. Modern analytical concepts may be useful, but they must not be projected backward without explanation.

Military events should be connected to relevant logistics, geography, weather, institutions, economics, politics, social structures, and technology. Avoid narratives in which victory is explained solely by courage, genius, weapon superiority, or national character.

## Chicago notes and bibliography

All articles must use the Chicago Notes and Bibliography system.

### Endnotes

- Use consecutively numbered endnotes.
- Place note markers after the relevant sentence or clause and after punctuation.
- Give a full citation the first time a source appears.
- Use a shortened author-title-page citation for later references.
- Do not use “ibid.”; use shortened citations for clarity and durability.
- Include precise page numbers or page ranges whenever the source provides stable pagination.
- Cite every direct quotation.
- Cite distinctive factual claims, disputed assertions, quantitative claims, interpretive arguments, and close paraphrases.
- A single note may support a coherent paragraph or cluster of closely related claims when the source coverage is clear.
- Avoid attaching notes mechanically to common knowledge or every sentence.

On the website, note markers should link to the corresponding numbered endnote, and each endnote should provide a return link when practical. Use semantic, accessible HTML and stable unique IDs.

### Bibliography

End each article with a complete bibliography, alphabetized by the author’s surname or by the responsible institution. Include every substantive source cited in the endnotes. Do not list works that were merely discovered but not used.

Format books, journal articles, chapters, archival material, official publications, and webpages according to current Chicago Notes and Bibliography conventions. Include DOI links or stable institutional URLs when appropriate. Access dates should be used for undated or changeable web content.

### Quotations and copyright

Quote only when the original wording is historically or analytically important. Prefer accurate paraphrase for ordinary factual material. Keep quotations proportionate to the article and never reproduce extensive copyrighted passages. Preserve original spelling only when it serves a clear purpose; otherwise explain silent modernization or editorial changes when relevant.

## Article structure

Every draft should normally contain:

1. A clear, descriptive title.
2. A concise subtitle or lead explaining the subject’s significance.
3. An introduction that establishes context, scope, and thesis.
4. Logically ordered sections with descriptive headings.
5. A conclusion that answers the article’s central question and explains its significance without merely repeating the introduction.
6. Numbered endnotes.
7. A Chicago-style bibliography.

Battle narratives should do more than recount movements. Explain objectives, forces, command decisions, terrain or maritime conditions, logistics, intelligence, tactical systems, uncertainty, and consequences.

Leader studies should avoid simple praise or condemnation. Evaluate the information available to the subject at the time, institutional constraints, alternatives, execution, and the difference between decision quality and eventual outcome.

Technology and weapon-development articles should distinguish design intent, technical characteristics, production, doctrine, training, reliability, logistics, actual combat performance, and later reputation.

## Voice and style

Write in clear American English for an informed general audience. The tone should be authoritative but not overconfident, lively but not sensational, and analytical without becoming needlessly abstract.

- Prefer specific nouns and active verbs.
- Define specialist terms when first introduced.
- Introduce individuals with enough context for readers to understand their role.
- Use ranks, unit names, ship names, aircraft names, dates, and geographic names consistently.
- Italicize ship names and book or journal titles as appropriate.
- Avoid clichés, sweeping national stereotypes, unsupported superlatives, and claims that an event “changed history forever.”
- Avoid fabricated scenes, imagined dialogue, and novelistic access to an actor’s thoughts.
- Do not mention the automated drafting process in the article.

## Accuracy and fact-checking pass

After completing the draft, perform a separate fact-checking pass. At minimum, verify:

- names, titles, ranks, and commands;
- dates and chronology;
- locations and geographic relationships;
- unit, ship, aircraft, and weapon designations;
- force strengths and casualty figures;
- technical specifications;
- quotations and their attribution;
- claims about motives, causation, significance, and “firsts”;
- every endnote against the cited source;
- agreement between endnotes and bibliography.

Create a private research note identifying any remaining uncertainty, contested interpretation, or source limitation. Do not conceal unresolved problems with confident prose.

## Website preparation

Match the established Rockhopper 1776 Writings presentation unless the site owner instructs otherwise.

The prepared draft should include:

- a standalone article page using the existing article layout and styling;
- a breadcrumb back to Writings;
- the established topic metadata and lead treatment;
- a table of contents linked to unique section IDs;
- a reading-time estimate based on article prose;
- semantic headings and accessible links;
- Chicago endnotes and bibliography inside the article body;
- a new Writings-page card placed above older articles;
- a concise card description consistent with the article’s thesis;
- a stable, descriptive lowercase URL slug.

Do not update homepage featured content unless specifically instructed. Do not alter existing articles, cards, navigation, or shared styles merely to accommodate a new draft unless a necessary change is clearly identified in the review report.

### Publication date during review

The scheduled task prepares a draft; it does not publish. Treat the publication date as pending until the site owner approves the article. The review report may suggest a date, but the final article and Writings card must use the actual date on which the approved article is committed for publication.

If the website files require a date during the review stage, use an unmistakable editorial placeholder and call it out in the report. Never allow a placeholder date to be published.

## Required workflow

Follow this sequence for every automated article:

1. Read this entire standard and record that it was reviewed.
2. Inspect the existing Writings page and articles for subject coverage, style, and duplication.
3. Select an in-scope topic and determine the article type, military domain, era, central question, and appropriate length.
4. Build a preliminary source list and verify that adequate scholarship is accessible.
5. Stop if the source base does not meet this standard.
6. Form a focused research question and provisional thesis.
7. Research the topic, keeping private notes that distinguish evidence, interpretation, and uncertainty.
8. Create an outline proportionate to the target length.
9. Draft the article without citations first only if source mapping is maintained; otherwise draft with notes as claims are written.
10. Add and verify Chicago endnotes and bibliography.
11. Perform the separate accuracy and citation audit.
12. Perform an editorial pass for organization, clarity, repetition, tone, and length.
13. Prepare the article page and Writings card in the isolated task worktree.
14. Validate document structure, table-of-contents links, internal links, dates, reading time, encoding, and the repository diff.
15. Produce the required review report and alert the site owner.

## Review-only boundary

The initial automation is strictly review-gated.

It must not:

- push a branch;
- push or merge to `main`;
- publish the article;
- open a ready-to-merge pull request;
- change the live website;
- delete or rewrite unrelated files;
- resolve uncertainty by silently lowering this standard.

The task may prepare files in its isolated worktree so the site owner can review the complete article and card. Publication requires a separate, explicit instruction from the site owner after review.

## Mandatory stop conditions

Stop the task and report the reason if any of the following occurs:

- this standard cannot be read in full;
- the proposed subject falls outside the chronological or editorial scope;
- the topic substantially duplicates an existing article;
- adequate authoritative sources cannot be accessed;
- a central source or citation cannot be verified;
- the evidence is too weak to support the proposed thesis;
- the article would require fabricated detail or unsupported certainty;
- repository conflicts or unrelated changes make safe preparation impossible;
- validation reveals a material problem that cannot be corrected confidently;
- the task would need to publish, push, or modify live content without explicit approval.

A stopped task is a successful safeguard, not an invitation to improvise around the problem.

## Required review report

Every completed scheduled run must alert the site owner and report:

- confirmation that this standard was read before research;
- proposed title;
- historical era and date range;
- military domain and article type;
- central research question and thesis;
- article word count and estimated reading time;
- number and types of sources used;
- principal primary and secondary sources;
- contested interpretations represented;
- assumptions, conjectures, and unresolved uncertainties;
- files created or changed;
- validation performed and its results;
- any editorial decisions requiring human review;
- publication status stated exactly as: **Not published — awaiting editorial approval.**

The report must be candid. It should draw the reviewer’s attention to the weakest evidentiary point, the most consequential interpretive judgment, and any citation that deserves manual inspection.

## Final governing principle

Accuracy takes precedence over cadence, novelty, length, and publication. When the evidence does not support a confident article, the correct result is to stop, explain why, and leave the site unchanged.
