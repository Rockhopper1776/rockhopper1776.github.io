Between the Springs and the Lake: Saladin and the Battle of Hattin

Saladin did more than catch a thirsty army in the July heat. He made movement and water the terms of battle, then kept narrowing Frankish options until infantry and cavalry could no longer fight as one force.

Introduction

The Battle of Hattin is remembered through physical torment. On July 4, 1187, the army of the kingdom of Jerusalem stood on dry ground west of the Sea of Galilee, surrounded by Saladin's forces and cut off from dependable water. Arrows thinned its horses. Smoke from burning grass drifted across men already exhausted by heat and a day of marching. Infantry crowded onto the twin heights called the Horns of Hattin while mounted troops made desperate attacks below. By the end, King Guy of Jerusalem and many leading barons were prisoners, the relic of the True Cross was lost, and the kingdom's army had ceased to exist as a coherent field force. If the battle is told only from that final position, its outcome can seem preordained.

That sense of inevitability is misleading. Frankish armies had crossed exposed country under mounted attack before, using close-ordered infantry to shield their horses while knights discouraged an enemy from closing. Guy himself had kept such a fighting march intact against Saladin in 1183. Nor had Saladin's earlier invasions consistently produced the decisive battle he wanted. Hattin was not the automatic result of one army possessing horse archers and the other wearing armor in summer. It followed from Saladin's success in changing the conditions under which a familiar Frankish system had to operate.1

Changing those conditions required more than luring a reckless opponent away from a spring. Saladin made Tiberias a politically difficult objective to abandon, contested the Frankish march from the Springs of Saffuriya, covered the approaches to reliable water, and sustained pressure while his own forces replenished water and arrows. Heat, distrust, terrain, and missile losses became decisive because each reduced the army's ability to answer the others. The surviving Latin and Muslim narratives disagree too sharply to support a minute-by-minute reconstruction, but they do reveal that broader mechanism. It was already at work before the field army ever saw the Horns.

Tiberias as a Lever

At the well-watered Springs of Saffuriya, the Frankish host assembled after Saladin brought forces from Egypt, Syria, and Mesopotamia into the region. An attack on this concentrated position offered him no easy prospect of a decisive battle. In previous campaigns the Franks had denied Saladin a favorable battle by staying near supplies and refusing to be drawn into a vulnerable pursuit. He therefore crossed the Jordan, occupied ground around Kafr Sabt that helped control the roads, and on July 2 attacked Tiberias. The town fell quickly, although its citadel continued to resist under Countess Eschiva of Tripoli. The move put a fortified place, a noble household, and Saladin's army before the Frankish council at once. It did not dictate their answer, but it forced them to choose.2

The choice was dangerous because it had to be debated within a coalition still burdened by mistrust. Raymond III of Tripoli, Eschiva's husband, had only recently been reconciled with Guy after a bitter political rupture. Latin and Muslim accounts preserve elaborate council scenes in which Raymond urges the army to remain at Saffuriya, while Gerard of Ridefort or other opponents persuade the king to march. The speeches are not transcripts, and the narratives distribute blame according to later loyalties. The dilemma common to these accounts is more dependable than their reported dialogue: remaining near the springs would preserve the army and compel Saladin to sustain his invasion, while refusing aid to Tiberias would expose Guy to accusations that fear or Raymond's influence had overruled royal duty. Military counsel could therefore be heard as a bid for influence before it was judged on operational grounds. A king who accepted Raymond's restraint might preserve his army, but he would also appear to entrust the kingdom's defense to a magnate who had recently challenged him.3

Politics did not make the march irrational. Tiberias mattered, its citadel was still holding, and the road led toward sources that appeared capable of supporting an army. A successful advance might relieve the fortress or make Saladin fight the concentrated Frankish force on less favorable terms. Yet the consequences of failure were unusually severe. The mobilization had drawn troops from towns and castles and used money sent by Henry II of England to hire additional men. It had created the kingdom's strongest striking force by concentrating much of its mobile defense in one body. Concentration therefore magnified command risk: a reverse that an ordinary raiding force might absorb could now remove the kingdom's means of answering Saladin elsewhere. When Guy ordered that body east on the morning of July 3, a disputed council decision became a physical problem of pace, formation, and water.4

The Road Becomes the Battlefield

Once the army exchanged Saffuriya's abundant springs for the road to Tiberias, its marching formation became its principal defense. Infantry and missile troops had to remain close enough to screen horses and mounted men from arrows. The cavalry, in turn, had to threaten any force that pressed too near the foot soldiers. Baggage, rear guard, and leading divisions all had to keep a pace slow enough to preserve that relationship. This system had worked in 1183, but on July 3 it was tested by heat and by mounted attacks aimed less at closing for a direct engagement than at slowing the column, stretching its parts, and consuming its time.5

That defensive relationship could endure only if the column reached water before the march exhausted men and animals. The sources mention several possible routes and disagree about some turns, but they consistently describe harassment, thirst, and a force unable to make the expected progress. The spring at Ayn Turan has often been cast as the scene of Guy's supposedly fatal mistake: the army reached water, failed to use it, and marched into destruction. Benjamin Kedar's study of the battlefield complicates that verdict. A modern July measurement found the spring producing only about 180 liters an hour, far below the output at Saffuriya or the springs near Hattin. Medieval flow cannot be recovered exactly, yet the comparison makes it unlikely that Ayn Turan could have watered thousands of men and animals. Leaving it was a risk, not inexplicable blindness.6

Turan therefore offered relief to part of a column rather than a secure base for the whole army. As attacks harried the rear, the Franks halted before reaching either the lake or the more dependable springs around Hattin. Kedar places the overnight camp near Maskana, a road junction where traces of a pool and cistern suggest that limited water may once have been available. Whether that pool held water in July 1187 is unknown, and the exact camp cannot be fixed. Even so, the halt need not imply a free choice of barren ground. It was the provisional end of a march whose pace had already been constrained by enemy attack. Failure to reach dependable water gave Saladin the night in which to turn delay into containment.7

A Siege Without Walls

By night, the exposed camp resembled a siege without fortifications. Saladin's wings covered approaches to water while his center pressed the Frankish rear. Ibn al-Athir reports that Muslim troops blocked movement toward the lake, and several traditions describe water being carried from it to Saladin's army. Arrows were redistributed during the night. The Franks could neither replenish on the same scale nor recover their marching order without again moving under attack. Water denial and encirclement were not separate devices. Together they let one army renew the pressure that the other had spent the day absorbing.8

Unequal endurance made every additional burden more costly. Latin and Muslim accounts agree that dry vegetation burned and that smoke and heat worsened the Frankish condition. They do not agree about when the fire began, who ordered it, or whether the apparently single fire represents several episodes compressed into one dramatic scene. Whatever its timing or origin, the burning grass did not suddenly win a battle that had otherwise been even. It added smoke, fear, and heat to a force that was already short of water, was losing horses, and still had to move to escape the ring.9

Those pressures operated across ground that was difficult for mounted troops to use freely. Archaeological study around the Horns has identified older field walls, stone piles, slopes, and route constraints that could break the continuity of a charge or channel movement. Such features help explain why local choices became costly, but they do not prove that Saladin had surveyed a perfect trap or planned every tactical effect before Guy left Saffuriya. His larger advantage came from repeated intervention: holding roads, pressing the column, covering water, renewing missiles, and reacting to attempted movement. Terrain made those actions more effective. They became decisive only when the Frankish formation could no longer keep its parts mutually supporting.10

When the Fighting March Failed

That loss of mutual support, rather than thirst alone, marked the fighting march's failure. The author of the Latin Libellus recognized the underlying relationship even while assigning moral blame: infantry and cavalry were meant to protect each other, yet the foot soldiers climbed toward the Horns and would not return when the mounted men called for them. Other sources place events differently, and no account establishes which of command failure, a search for refuge, missile losses, or pressure on broken ground first caused the separation. Operationally, each local attempt to survive reduced the force's shared capability. Infantry on higher ground had some protection, but horses and knights below lost their shield. Mounted attacks could open a temporary path, but they no longer restored the column.11

Separation also changed the meaning of escape. Raymond and a small mounted group broke through or passed out of the encirclement, but the accounts turn the movement into incompatible stories. Ibn al-Athir says Taqi al-Din opened a passage before closing ranks behind the riders. Old French versions describe a charge made on Guy's order, while the Libellus presents Raymond's men as cut off from the rest. Later accusations could make survival look like treachery, especially because Raymond had opposed the march. The battlefield effect requires no verdict on his motive: part of the mounted force left the enclosure, and the gap that admitted it did not remain open for the army behind.12

The troops left inside the enclosure still resisted. Ruined walls on the Horns suggest why infantry might have chosen the heights as a defensible refuge rather than simply fleeing there in panic. Below them, mounted men regrouped near the royal tents and launched attacks that at least one Muslim tradition remembered as dangerous. Ibn al-Athir, drawing on a story attributed to Saladin's son al-Afdal, describes Frankish charges driving Muslim troops back toward the sultan before the king's tent finally fell. The precise number and order of those attacks are uncertain, but the episode keeps the final stage from becoming an effortless massacre. Frankish resistance continued after the common formation had collapsed, but the army could no longer recover collective maneuver.13

The Battle We Cannot Reassemble

The persistence of that resistance is clearer than its sequence. Latin accounts agree on heat, thirst, harassment, fire, and failing coordination, but they move Raymond's departure, the infantry's ascent, and other episodes into different orders. Their explanations also reflect a kingdom searching for culprits among Guy, Raymond, Gerard, and the competing factions around them. Muslim narratives preserve command actions and the alarm caused by Frankish attacks, yet they were shaped by a victory tradition that emphasized divine favor and employed polished speeches. Even the letter to Archumbald, written within two months of Hattin, compresses catastrophe for an urgent western audience rather than offering a neutral operations record. Blending these voices into one smooth eyewitness narrative would conceal the evidence problem rather than solve it.14

Those contradictions still leave a firm operational spine, but not a story built around one fatal order, one timed fire, or one act of betrayal. They show Saladin retaining the initiative as Frankish access to water narrowed and the combined-arms formation deteriorated. By the time individual movements replaced coordinated action, he could determine which problem the Franks faced next.15

A Kingdom Without Its Field Army

Once coordinated resistance collapsed, the defeat reached far beyond the ground around the Horns. Captives included Guy, his brother Aimery, the constable, many leading nobles, Reynald of Chatillon, and large numbers of Templars and Hospitallers. Saladin struck Reynald before men around him completed the killing, and many of the military-order prisoners were executed. Other soldiers were killed, taken prisoner, or dispersed, while the True Cross passed into Saladin's hands. Exact army and casualty totals in the narratives are too unstable to repeat as facts. The more consequential loss was institutional: the kingdom had lost much of its mobile army, command network, military-order leadership, and symbolic center in the same encounter.16

That institutional loss converted a battlefield victory into a campaign of conquest. Saladin moved rapidly to take Acre and a succession of coastal and inland places whose defenders could no longer expect relief from a field army. Jerusalem surrendered on October 2. Yet Hattin was not an automatic end to Frankish power. Tyre held, scattered forces regrouped, and news of Hattin and Saladin's subsequent conquests helped call forth the Third Crusade. The battle opened an extraordinary opportunity that Saladin still had to exploit. Surviving ports limited what even this victory could settle, but the kingdom's exposure showed how much had depended on the army destroyed at Hattin.17

Conclusion

Because so much depended on that army, its destruction requires more than the familiar shorthand of thirst, mistrust, smoke, and arrows. Those conditions were real, but none explains the defeat in isolation. Political mistrust made a relief march harder to reject; the decision to leave Saffuriya exposed a formation dependent on pace and mutual protection; harassment turned distance into delay; delay made access to water urgent; containment made resupply unequal; and prolonged pressure pulled infantry and cavalry apart. Each link carried the consequence of the one before it into the next.

Seen in that sequence, Saladin's achievement was neither a single inspired trick nor the passive harvest of Guy's mistake. He created the dilemma at Tiberias, then made a sequence of operational decisions that compounded his advantages as Frankish options contracted. The sources cannot recover every charge or motive, but they support a broader judgment: Hattin became decisive because Saladin kept converting political, geographic, logistical, and tactical pressure into the failure of an entire military system.
