The campaign behind the battle

In 1759, France prepared to carry the Seven Years’ War onto British shores. Off the coast of Brittany, Admiral Sir Edward Hawke’s fleet watched Brest, seeking to intercept the French warships on which the invasion depended. Keeping that fleet at sea required a continuous effort to supply provisions, repair damaged ships, care for sick crews, and gather reliable intelligence along an enemy coast.

The Long Watch follows this demanding campaign through the French fleet’s escape and Hawke’s pursuit into the dangerous waters of Quiberon Bay. Drawing on contemporary correspondence and historical accounts, it explores the decisions of British and French commanders, the work that kept their ships ready, and the uncertainty that accompanied orders from shore. The battle of November 20 emerges from months of preparation, endurance, and choices made with imperfect knowledge.

Command, endurance, and the war at sea

Across nine chapters, the book brings the campaign into focus through:

  • French invasion plans and the British effort to defend home waters while fighting a global war
  • Hawke, his captains, and their French opponents as they weighed orders, intelligence, and opportunities
  • The practical demands of blockade, from food and repairs to reconnaissance, illness, and heavy weather
  • The fighting at Quiberon Bay, its difficult aftermath, and the campaign’s place in Britain’s wider wartime success

Written for readers of naval and military history, the narrative explains unfamiliar practices through the work of officers and sailors. Chapter endnotes and a bibliography provide a path into the sources behind the account.
