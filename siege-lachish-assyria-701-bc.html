The Siege of Lachish, 701 BC: How Assyria Took a Fortress

In 701 BC, Sennacherib, king of the Neo-Assyrian Empire, invaded the smaller kingdom of Judah after its ruler, Hezekiah, rebelled against Assyrian control. His army attacked Lachish, a fortified Judahite city in the low hills southwest of Jerusalem, and built a stone ramp up the side of the mound. The ramp survives beside the ruined defenses, and reliefs from Sennacherib's palace show how he later presented the victory.

Introduction

Lachish belonged to Judah and stood on a high mound in the Shephelah, the low hill country between the Mediterranean coastal plain and the Judean highlands. Roads through this region led east toward Jerusalem. Its position and fortifications made Lachish one of Judah's principal administrative and military centers outside the capital.

Sennacherib ruled the Neo-Assyrian Empire, which was centered in northern Mesopotamia and dominated much of the Near East. Assyrian kings governed some conquered lands directly and left others under local rulers who paid tribute. Hezekiah ruled one such kingdom, Judah, from Jerusalem. When he rejected Assyrian authority and joined a regional revolt, Sennacherib marched west in 701 BC to restore control.

Assyrian scribes and sculptors recorded the campaign in royal inscriptions and palace reliefs; Judahite narratives place the Assyrian king at Lachish. Modern excavators have uncovered the ramp, weapons, damaged defenses, and buildings destroyed by fire. These records document the siege in unusual detail for an Iron Age battle.1

At Lachish, the Assyrians chose the southwestern corner of the mound, built a stone ramp toward the wall, and supported their siege engines with archers and slingers. The surviving evidence does not reveal the ramp's exact original shape, how long its construction took, how many engines reached the wall, or the order in which the defenses failed. Although the palace relief adds soldiers, weapons, and captives to the account, it cannot answer those questions by itself.

Judah Rebels against Assyria

For decades before the siege, Assyrian kings had forced the smaller kingdoms of the Levant to accept direct rule or pay tribute. Hezekiah inherited Judah's obligations from his father Ahaz, who had sought Assyrian protection. When Sennacherib's father, Sargon II, died unexpectedly in 705 BC, revolts spread through the empire. Hezekiah joined the resistance, imprisoned the pro-Assyrian king of Ekron, and looked toward Egypt and Nubia for military aid.2

In 701 BC, Sennacherib brought his army west to suppress the revolts. His third campaign moved through Phoenicia and Philistia before defeating an Egyptian and Nubian force near Eltekeh, restoring the displaced ruler of Ekron, and entering Judah. The Assyrian account claims the capture of forty-six fortified cities and uncounted smaller settlements; most surviving copies give forty-six, although two preserve different totals. It also lists the methods used against those places: ramps, battering rams, infantry assaults, sapping, breaching, and siege engines.3

Although Sennacherib's summary does not name Lachish among the forty-six cities, a Judahite account places him there and a cuneiform caption on the palace relief identifies the city. The Assyrian king probably used Lachish as a base and sent another force to confront Hezekiah at Jerusalem. By taking the fortress, he removed one of Judah's strongest regional centers; Hezekiah remained confined in his capital.4

Written Accounts, Reliefs, and Excavations

Sennacherib's royal inscriptions describe the whole campaign rather than the fighting at one city. They list siege methods, conquered settlements, deportees, and tribute, but they do not assign each operation to a particular place. The biblical narratives preserve Sennacherib's presence at Lachish and the negotiations over Jerusalem. Their authors were concerned with Hezekiah, Jerusalem, and divine deliverance, not with a technical account of the assault on Lachish.5

Several years after the campaign, sculptors covered a room in Sennacherib's Southwest Palace at Nineveh with a relief sequence nearly twenty-seven meters long. Soldiers advance toward the city, siege engines attack the walls, captives and booty move away, and Sennacherib receives the results from his throne. Archaeologist David Ussishkin argues that the artists preserved recognizable features of Lachish and its terrain. Christoph Uehlinger cautions that the sculptors also used familiar Assyrian motifs, altered scale, and arranged the action for a palace audience. Made to commemorate the victory for that audience, the relief does not reproduce the battlefield as an eyewitness would have seen it.6

Archaeologists use the name Level III for the eighth-century Judahite city destroyed in 701 BC. At its southwestern corner they found the stone siege ramp, concentrations of arrowheads and sling stones, and debris from damaged fortifications. These remains locate the main attack and confirm fighting around the head of the ramp. Later inhabitants removed stones for new construction, early excavations disturbed parts of the area, and individual weapons cannot always be assigned to attacker or defender. The surviving ramp identifies the attack site; disturbance and later building have obscured the sequence of the fighting.7

Why the Assyrians Attacked from the Southwest

Most of the mound rose steeply beneath Lachish's walls. The city wall rested on substantial stone foundations and rose in mud brick above them, while a large gate complex controlled the western entrance. At the southwestern corner, lower ground connected the mound to a neighboring hill, and the road to the gate climbed through the same sector. That approach gave the Assyrians space to gather workers, missile troops, and siege engines below one part of the defenses.8

The surviving ramp lies against this southwestern slope and identifies it as the main attack route. Ussishkin places Sennacherib's camp on the hill across the low ground and interprets the relief as a view from near the king's position. No camp remains have been found to confirm that reconstruction, and scholars disagree over how precisely the carved city matches the excavated walls and gate. Whatever the camp's location, Assyrian soldiers and laborers approached the fortifications from the southwest.

Building the Assyrian Siege Ramp

Assyrian laborers built the ramp from local limestone pieces dumped against the slope rather than laid in regular courses. A modern sample of two hundred stones averaged about 6.4 kilograms, a weight one person could carry. Workers extended the pile uphill until its surface provided a route for men and heavy engines. The work required a large supply of stone, organized transport, and protection from defenders above.9

The lower part of the ramp survives, but later construction removed much of its top and sides. Ussishkin reconstructed a short, broad ramp; later calculations based on his alternatives produced lengths between about fifty-seven and sixty-eight meters. A 2021 photogrammetric study proposed a narrower route of roughly eighty meters with a gentler slope. Its authors calculated that the ramp contained about 19,456 metric tons of stone and identified a scarred outcrop south of the mound as a possible quarry.10

The same study estimated that laborers working in organized chains could have finished the structure in twenty to twenty-five days. That timetable depends on the proposed eighty-meter route, continuous shifts, and assumptions about how quickly workers could quarry and move the stones. No Assyrian text identifies the workforce, quarry, schedule, or direction of construction at Lachish. These calculations offer one plausible timetable, but no evidence shows how Sennacherib's army actually organized the work.

The Assyrians also needed a surface firm enough for wheeled engines. An earlier reconstruction gave the ramp a mortared cap, although the plaster cited as evidence may belong to the city rebuilt after the siege. The palace relief shows long horizontal pieces beneath the engines, which the 2021 study interprets as wooden planks laid over an earthen surface. The carving and the disputed plaster do not prove that the Assyrians finished the roadway with planks.

Attacking the Wall

In the palace relief, Assyrian archers and slingers shoot toward the wall while wheeled rams strike the defenses. The missile troops cover the engines and the men working around them. Large shields protect some attackers; above them, defenders shoot arrows, throw stones, and cast burning material at the machines. Seven engines appear in the carving, but the sculptors repeated figures and placed several stages of the attack in one view. The carving therefore provides no reliable count of engines operating at one time.11

Concentrations of arrowheads and sling stones found near the ramp confirm missile fighting in this area. Drilled stones bearing traces of cord may have belonged to defensive devices used against the engines. An iron chain found nearby has produced three explanations: defenders may have used it to catch a ram, it may have suspended a beam that defenders dropped onto a ram, or it may have formed part of an Assyrian engine. Because excavators found the chain beside the ramp, it probably belonged to the fighting there; its exact use remains unknown.

Inside Lachish, excavators found smashed pottery beneath burned and collapsed mud brick, along with ash, sling stones, and arrowheads in buildings destroyed during the Assyrian attack. In the palace relief, engines strike the walls while defenders fight, captives leave, prisoners are killed, and booty is carried toward the king. Because the sculptors placed different moments in one composition, the relief does not show the order of the attacks that broke the defenses.12

Captives and the Palace Reliefs

After the attack, the relief shows Assyrian soldiers carrying spoils and officials supervising captives as families leave the city. Other scenes show executions and prisoners brought before Sennacherib. Assyrian kings used deportation to punish rebellion and redistribute conquered populations. Sennacherib's claim that 200,150 people were removed from Judah applies to the whole campaign and cannot be used as a count of Lachish's population or of the people represented in the relief.13

Sennacherib displayed the reliefs in a room reached through monumental doorways guarded by carved winged figures. Within the room, the scenes led viewers from the Assyrian advance through the city's capture to the enthroned king. The royal inscriptions included Lachish within a general account of conquered Judahite cities; the sculptors instead devoted an entire room to this victory. The inscriptions credited Sennacherib in a campaign summary, while the reliefs gave visitors a long and carefully arranged view of the people, weapons, and punishments involved.14

Archaeologists can compare details in the carvings with the excavated ramp, the weapons found nearby, and the destruction inside the city. The arrangement of the scenes cannot establish the scale of objects, the number of engines, or which actions occurred at the same time. The reliefs show what Assyrian artists and officials chose to present several years after the army captured Lachish.

Conclusion

The surviving ramp shows where Assyrian laborers built a stone approach up the southwestern slope toward the wall. Archers and slingers supported the assault, and siege engines attacked the fortifications. Excavated weapons and burned buildings confirm heavy fighting and destruction in 701 BC.

Scholars have proposed several estimates for the ramp's length, stone volume, labor requirements, and construction time. Later building removed too much of the structure for any one design to be confirmed. The palace relief shows weapons, soldiers, defenders, and captives. Its scale and sequence were arranged to present a royal victory. The Assyrian attack ended with the capture and destruction of Lachish.
