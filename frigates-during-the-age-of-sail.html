Frigates during the Age of Sail

Fast, powerful, and capable of operating far beyond the battle fleet, the frigate became the indispensable cruiser of eighteenth- and early nineteenth-century naval warfare.

Introduction

The great ships of the line have always dominated popular accounts of warfare during the Age of Sail. These towering vessels formed the battle fleets that decided such famous contests as the Nile and Trafalgar. However, the ordinary work of naval warfare was most often performed by a smaller and more versatile class of warship. Frigates scouted for enemy fleets, carried dispatches, protected merchant convoys, raided hostile coasts, and hunted enemy commerce. If the ship of the line won command of the sea, the frigate allowed a navy to exercise it.

The Birth of the Frigate

The word “frigate” possessed no precise meaning during the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries. It could describe a Mediterranean oared vessel, a lightly armed Dunkirk privateer, or simply a warship valued for speed. The recognizable sailing frigate emerged during the first half of the eighteenth century, when French naval constructors sought a more effective vessel for commerce raiding and colonial service.

The French Médée, designed by Blaise Ollivier and launched at Brest in 1741, is commonly identified as the prototype of the true frigate. She carried her principal battery on a single continuous upper deck, while the deck below was left unarmed and used for berthing the crew. This placed the gunports safely above the water, allowing them to be opened in rough weather. The result combined speed, weatherliness, and useful firepower to a degree that older cruisers could not match.

The new French ships proved their worth during the War of the Austrian Succession. After the Royal Navy captured Tygre, Rear Admiral George Anson urged the Admiralty to have her lines copied “with the utmost exactness.” British constructors adapted the concept to their own requirements, producing Unicorn and Lyme in 1748. Other navies soon followed.

A Ship between Two Worlds

By the middle of the eighteenth century, a frigate was generally understood to be a three-masted, square-rigged warship carrying its main armament on one gun deck, with lighter weapons on the quarterdeck and forecastle. Most Royal Navy frigates were fifth- or sixth-rates. They were too lightly armed to stand in the line of battle, but far more powerful than sloops, brigs, and cutters.

Armament increased steadily as rival navies sought an advantage. Early British frigates commonly carried twenty-eight 9-pounders. During the Seven Years’ War, 32-gun frigates armed with 12-pounders became important. By the American Revolution, 36- and 38-gun ships carrying long 18-pounders had appeared. Short-ranged but powerful carronades later made their actual batteries still heavier than their official ratings suggested.

There was no single perfect design. French frigates often emphasized speed, while British vessels generally possessed stronger hulls, deeper holds, and more space for stores. The difference reflected strategy as much as national skill. A French commerce raider benefited from speed; a British frigate maintaining a blockade or protecting distant trade required endurance in poor weather.

The Eyes of the Fleet

The frigate’s most celebrated role was reconnaissance. Sailing ahead or to windward of the battle fleet, frigates searched for enemy ships and reported their number, position, and course. During battle they repeated signals through the smoke, rescued men, and took crippled ships in tow. Their value led to their famous description as the “eyes of the fleet.”

Frigates also made sustained blockades possible. While ships of the line remained farther offshore, frigates watched hostile anchorages and hurried back with news of any movement. Admiral Edward Hawke’s blockade of Brest in 1759 depended upon them. When Hubert de Conflans finally escaped, the watching frigates carried the warning that sent Hawke racing toward Quiberon Bay.

Fleet service represented only one portion of their duties. Frigates escorted convoys, pursued privateers, transported diplomats, delivered dispatches, and raided coastal shipping and fortifications. Because they could cruise independently for long periods, they extended naval power into waters where no battle fleet could remain. A single frigate off a colonial port might protect friendly commerce, interrupt enemy trade, gather intelligence, and exert diplomatic pressure at the same time.

The Frigate Captain’s War

For an ambitious officer, command of a frigate was one of the navy’s most desirable appointments. A frigate captain was frequently entrusted with broad orders and expected to exercise his own judgment. Independent cruising also offered chances to capture enemy merchantmen and privateers. Under the prize system, the proceeds were divided among the officers and crew, allowing a successful cruise to transform a captain’s fortune and supplement a sailor’s modest pay.

The rewards should not obscure the hardships. A typical frigate carried two hundred to three hundred men within a cramped wooden hull. They handled the sails, maintained the rigging, operated the guns, and kept the ship seaworthy during months away from port. Speed and maneuverability depended as much upon their training as upon the vessel’s design.

Single-ship actions consequently became tests of seamanship, gunnery, discipline, and command, making heroes of officers such as Edward Pellew, Thomas Cochrane, and Philip Broke. Nevertheless, these duels were only the most visible portion of the frigate’s war. Its greater contribution lay in the ceaseless work of patrol, blockade, communication, and trade protection.

The Heavy Frigate

The final major development of the sailing frigate came from the United States Navy. Unable to afford a conventional battle fleet, the United States authorized six frigates in 1794. Joshua Humphreys designed the largest—including United States, Constitution, and President—to overpower ordinary frigates while escaping ships of the line. Their long hulls, heavy live-oak construction, large crews, and 24-pounder main batteries made them formidable cruisers.

During the War of 1812, the victories of Constitution over Guerriere and Java, and of United States over Macedonian, demonstrated their advantage over conventional British 18-pounder ships. These victories did not overturn British control of the Atlantic, but they inflicted a psychological blow and accelerated the movement toward heavier frigates. Steam propulsion, shell-firing guns, and iron construction eventually transformed the type, but its essential purpose endured.

Conclusion

No other warship of the sailing era combined so many functions within a single hull. Frigates could not defeat a battle fleet or permanently control a hostile coast, but they carried naval power rapidly and economically across the globe. They found the enemy, guarded trade, delivered orders, gathered intelligence, and kept pressure upon an opponent between the great battles. The frigate was not merely a smaller companion to the ship of the line. It was the vessel that made sea power useful from one day to the next.
