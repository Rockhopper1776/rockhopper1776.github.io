Before the Guns Opened: Readiness and the Battle of Tsushima

Admiral Tōgō Heihachirō accepted a dangerous turn in front of the Russian battle line on 27 May 1905. The Japanese victory that followed, however, had been prepared long before his ships came under fire.

Introduction

During the afternoon of 27 May 1905, Russian sailors watched the lead Japanese battleship swing across their course in the Korea Strait. Each ship behind it had to reach the same turning point, exposing the column to fire before its guns could bear effectively, so Vice Admiral Zinovy Rozhestvensky's leading battleships opened at once. For several minutes, the moment later celebrated as Tōgō's masterstroke looked less like a trap than an invitation to punish the Japanese fleet. 1

The invitation failed because the turn was only one part of the encounter. Japanese scouts had already located the Russians and reported their course by wireless. Tōgō approached with a fleet organized for common movement, practiced in concentrated gunfire, and supported by bases close to the battlefield. The Russians brought powerful new battleships and fired effectively at first, but they also brought ships of unequal speed, an awkward marching formation, and a command system that could not absorb the loss of its flagship. Japanese shells then converted those disadvantages into burning upper works, damaged steering, broken signals, and an increasingly disordered column. Tsushima became decisive when preparation allowed the Japanese to create one opportunity after another, while damage denied the Russians the same ability to recover.

A Fleet from Europe

Russia sent Rozhestvensky's Second Pacific Squadron from the Baltic in October 1904 because the war had gone badly in East Asia. Japanese forces had contained the Russian squadron at Port Arthur and threatened the fortress itself. A second fleet might restore the balance at sea, relieve the port, or at least reach Vladivostok and preserve a naval force in the theater. Before Rozhestvensky had crossed the Indian Ocean, however, Port Arthur surrendered and its ships were lost. His voyage continued, but the mission narrowed to reaching Vladivostok through waters controlled by an enemy that now possessed no rival fleet to distract it.2

More than seven months at sea altered what that mission required. Coal had to be acquired despite diplomatic pressure, loaded repeatedly, and carried wherever space could be found, while machinery needed maintenance far from a secure Russian dockyard. The squadron paused off Madagascar, later absorbed Nebogatov's reinforcements in Southeast Asia, and received news of defeat while officers were already uncertain about the value of continuing. Vladimir Semenoff, who joined Rozhestvensky's flagship Knyaz Suvorov after surviving Port Arthur, described the resulting arguments as well as the strain. His account is retrospective and intensely personal, but it captures a fleet whose crews understood that arrival alone had become an achievement.3

Despite the burdens of distance, four new Borodino-class battleships gave Rozhestvensky a powerful core whose heavy guns could damage any Japanese ship. He still had to keep older battleships, cruisers, auxiliaries, and transports together, so the formation moved according to the capacities of dissimilar vessels. The tactical difficulty became visible on the morning of battle, when the fleet shifted between columns and line ahead while maintaining a northeastward course. That arrangement protected the voyage, but it complicated the transition from passage to combat.

The Fleet That Waited

Tōgō's Combined Fleet faced a different problem: it had to find the Russians before they slipped past Japan while keeping enough force together to destroy them. Geography narrowed the possible routes, for the Tsushima Strait offered the shortest approach to Vladivostok, while routes east of Japan would lengthen a voyage already limited by coal and mechanical wear. Tōgō therefore concentrated near the likely passage and used patrol lines to watch its approaches rather than keeping the battle fleet continuously at sea.4

Because Tōgō could wait near home waters, Japanese battleships and armored cruisers could replenish, repair, and train from nearby bases. Their officers had accumulated wartime experience at Port Arthur and in the Battle of the Yellow Sea, while the Naval Staff College and prewar exercises had encouraged common tactical methods. The fleet was not uniform, and Japanese shells and guns had their own limitations, but its principal divisions could maneuver at useful speeds without being tied to transports. High-explosive shells filled with Shimose powder, coupled with sensitive fuzes, also made exposed positions and combustible fittings especially vulnerable.5

Tōgō still had to fight an armored fleet whose armor-piercing shells remained dangerous when they struck. He also lacked precise knowledge of when or where the enemy would appear. Japanese patrols, communications, and basing resolved that uncertainty while keeping the striking force rested and concentrated.

Finding the Enemy

Shortly before dawn on 27 May, the auxiliary cruiser Shinano Maru sighted lights and then identified Russian ships. Its wireless report reached Japanese headquarters, where information from other scouts gradually established the enemy's position, course, and formation. Tōgō could sortie knowing that the Russians were in the strait. Rozhestvensky, lacking a comparable screen, received fragmentary sightings of Japanese cruisers but no equally clear picture of the force approaching beyond the horizon.6

The wireless reports gained value from a patrol scheme that assigned observers, a staff that combined their sightings, and a battle plan that directed the fleet's response. Japanese cruisers then maintained contact while Tōgō shaped his approach, reducing his uncertainty without requiring the battleships to reveal themselves too early. By early afternoon, the Russians were still steering toward Vladivostok, whereas the Japanese commander was choosing how to cross their path.

The Turn under Fire

Tōgō first appeared on the Russian side that offered an unpromising angle of attack. He then reversed course and led his battle line through a turn in succession, each ship following the flagship around nearly the same patch of water. The maneuver brought the Japanese onto a roughly parallel course ahead of the Russians, where they could concentrate on the leading ships. Before the turn was complete, however, it presented a conspicuous aiming point. Russian officers recognized the chance, and Suvorov opened fire at about 1:49 p.m. by the Russian reckoning.7

While the Japanese ships completed the turn, the first Russian salvoes showed that Rozhestvensky's gunners could act quickly and hit their targets, and that Tōgō's maneuver carried real danger. Semenoff recalled early hits and the tense interval before Japanese guns answered. Once the Japanese column completed its turn, its greater speed and more coherent formation changed the geometry. Tōgō could draw ahead, keep the Russian van under converging fire, and turn again when the columns began to separate. Higher speed let Tōgō draw ahead or reopen the range, while practiced division maneuvers kept the following ships with him.8

Fire and Command

Japanese gunnery first concentrated on Suvorov and Oslyabya, the most visible ships at the heads of the Russian columns. Hits accumulated above the waterline, tearing through bridges, signal positions, ventilators, and unarmored structures. The high-explosive shells started fires and filled exposed stations with fragments. Semenoff's close description of paint, wood, boats, and fittings feeding the flames conveys what armor statistics alone obscure: a battleship could remain afloat while losing the people and instruments needed to act as part of a fleet.9

The effects Semenoff recorded later sustained Russian arguments that defective shells, inferior armor, or unstable ships had decided the battle; historians have since revised several of those claims. Japanese ammunition devastated personnel and command spaces, while armor, flooding, fire, structural damage, and damage-control practice all shaped whether a ship survived. The historical question is therefore less which shell possessed a universal technical superiority than how repeated hits affected the two fleets' ability to keep fighting together.10

As Japanese fire continued against the van, Russia's ability to fight as a fleet deteriorated. Oslyabya left the line and sank, while Suvorov lost steering, fell away from the formation, and carried a badly wounded Rozhestvensky with it. Smoke obscured signals; following captains had to infer whether the flagship's movements were orders or damage. The lead passed from ship to ship as the Japanese continued to attack the van. What began as a gunnery advantage thus became a command problem, and the command problem made subsequent gunnery less coherent.11

The Battle Breaks Apart

Russian captains continued to resist after the command system began to fail. Their column turned, temporarily moved out of effective range, and at times forced Tōgō to recover contact. Individual ships continued to fire, while cruisers and destroyers protected the auxiliaries or attempted to follow the battleships. These intervals explain why the afternoon should not be compressed into a single perfect crossing of the Russian line. It was a sequence of approaches, turns, separations, and renewed concentrations, each easier for the Japanese because their divisions retained speed and tactical control.12

When Japanese divisions renewed contact, they found a weaker Russian formation. Imperator Aleksandr III and Borodino eventually sank; Suvorov, isolated and already wrecked, was finished by torpedoes. Rear Admiral Nikolai Nebogatov inherited command of the surviving battleships, but darkness did not restore the common direction lost during the afternoon. Some ships steered north, others turned away, and cruiser forces became separated. The fleet that had remained together from the Baltic could no longer agree on a path through the strait.

Night and Morning

Japanese destroyers and torpedo boats entered that disorder after dark. Smoke, confusion, and the need to avoid collision limited their results, so their main value came after gunfire had already damaged and dispersed the Russians. The attacks kept ships maneuvering when crews needed to fight fires and restore order. The threat divided Russian attention, inflicted additional damage, and made regrouping still harder. Nearby Japanese bases and the division of labor between heavy ships and light forces allowed Tōgō to renew the pursuit at daylight.13

On 28 May, Japanese forces surrounded Nebogatov's remnant. With his ships outnumbered, damaged, and unable to reach Vladivostok as a useful squadron, he surrendered. Other Russian vessels were sunk, captured, interned in neutral ports, or scattered; only a few completed the passage, while the Japanese battle fleet had lost no capital ship. Tōgō's official report presented the outcome as the product of imperial virtue and providence as well as naval action, language that served the victory's public meaning but cannot explain its mechanics.14

What Tsushima Proved, and What It Did Not

Contemporary naval officers searched the battle for a portable lesson. Richard Wainwright emphasized training, scouting, wireless coordination, and prearranged tactical stages. Seaton Schroeder warned against reducing the outcome to superior speed or torpedo attack. Japanese speed mattered because it permitted disengagement and renewed concentration; torpedoes mattered most after gunfire had damaged the Russian fleet. Both writers placed technology within the organizations and conditions that made it effective.15

The caution offered by Wainwright and Schroeder helps separate the conditions of battle from the actions that followed. The long Russian voyage increased maintenance, supply, and training burdens, but distance did not fire a shell or compel Rozhestvensky to lose control at a particular minute. Japanese preparation made the initial contact favorable, yet Tōgō still risked his fleet in the turn and had to regain contact later. Shell design magnified fire and fragmentation, though the destruction unfolded through cumulative damage rather than a single technical revelation. Japanese and Russian accounts instead show how those conditions interacted as the battle developed.

Tsushima's wider reputation also grew beyond what the battle itself could prove. Japan's victory demonstrated that an Asian state could defeat a European empire in modern war, encouraged national movements far from the strait, and influenced naval discussion before the launch of Dreadnought. The evidence for its military meaning was narrower: months of blockade, land fighting, finance, diplomacy, and attrition had shaped the forces that met on 27 May.16

Conclusion

The famous turn deserves attention because it placed Tōgō's line where Japanese guns could strike the Russian van. Its success depended on work less visible in a track chart. Patrols found the enemy; wireless reports assembled a usable picture; nearby bases preserved the battle fleet; training and organization allowed divisions to maneuver together; concentrated fire then damaged the structures through which Rozhestvensky commanded. When the Russian line bent away, the Japanese could find it again. When the Russian flagship fell out, no equally resilient system restored common action.

By the morning of 28 May, those differences had accumulated into surrender and dispersal. Tsushima was not decided before the guns opened, but the fleets arrived with unequal capacities to turn uncertainty into information, contact into coordinated fire, and temporary advantage into another attack. That sequence ended when Nebogatov surrendered the battleships still with him.
